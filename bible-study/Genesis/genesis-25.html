Genesis

Chapter Twenty-Five

King James Version
Then again Abraham took a wife, and her name was Keturah. 2And she bare him Zimran, and Jokshan, and Medan, and Midian, and Ishbak, and Shuah. 3And Jokshan begat Sheba, and Dedan. And the sons of Dedan were Asshurim, and Letushim, and Leummim. 4And the sons of Midian; Ephah, and Epher, and Hanoch, and Abida, and Eldaah. All these were the children of Keturah.

5And Abraham gave all that he had unto Isaac. 6But unto the sons of the concubines, which Abraham had, Abraham gave gifts, and sent them away from Isaac his son, while he yet lived, eastward, unto the east country.

7And these are the days of the years of Abraham's life which he lived, an hundred threescore and fifteen years. 8Then Abraham gave up the ghost, and died in a good old age, an old man, and full of years; and was gathered to his people. 9And his sons Isaac and Ishmael buried him in the cave of Machpelah, in the field of Ephron the son of Zohar the Hittite, which is before Mamre; 10The field which Abraham purchased of the sons of Heth: there was Abraham buried, and Sarah his wife. 11And it came to pass after the death of Abraham, that God blessed his son Isaac; and Isaac dwelt by the well Lahairoi.

12Now these are the generations of Ishmael, Abraham's son, whom Hagar the Egyptian, Sarah's handmaid, bare unto Abraham: 13And these are the names of the sons of Ishmael, by their names, according to their generations: the firstborn of Ishmael, Nebajoth; and Kedar, and Adbeel, and Mibsam, 14And Mishma, and Dumah, and Massa, 15Hadar, and Tema, Jetur, Naphish, and Kedemah: 16These are the sons of Ishmael, and these are their names, by their towns, and by their castles; twelve princes according to their nations.

17And these are the years of the life of Ishmael, an hundred and thirty and seven years: and he gave up the ghost and died; and was gathered unto his people. 18And they dwelt from Havilah unto Shur, that is before Egypt, as thou goest toward Assyria: and he died in the presence of all his brethren.

19And these are the generations of Isaac, Abraham's son: Abraham begat Isaac: 20And Isaac was forty years old when he took Rebekah to wife, the daughter of Bethuel the Syrian of Padanaram, the sister to Laban the Syrian. 21And Isaac intreated the LORD for his wife, because she was barren: and the LORD was intreated of him, and Rebekah his wife conceived. 22And the children struggled together within her; and she said, If it be so, why am I thus? And she went to enquire of the LORD.

23And the LORD said unto her, Two nations are in thy womb, and two manner of people shall be separated from thy bowels; and the one people shall be stronger than the other people; and the elder shall serve the younger. 24And when her days to be delivered were fulfilled, behold, there were twins in her womb. 25And the first came out red, all over like an hairy garment; and they called his name Esau. 26And after that came his brother out, and his hand took hold on Esau's heel; and his name was called Jacob: and Isaac was threescore years old when she bare them.

27And the boys grew: and Esau was a cunning hunter, a man of the field; and Jacob was a plain man, dwelling in tents. 28And Isaac loved Esau, because he did eat of his venison: but Rebekah loved Jacob.

29And Jacob sod pottage: and Esau came from the field, and he was faint: 30And Esau said to Jacob, Feed me, I pray thee, with that same red pottage; for I am faint: therefore was his name called Edom. 31And Jacob said, Sell me this day thy birthright. 32And Esau said, Behold, I am at the point to die: and what profit shall this birthright do to me? 33And Jacob said, Swear to me this day; and he sware unto him: and he sold his birthright unto Jacob. 34Then Jacob gave Esau bread and pottage of lentiles; and he did eat and drink, and rose up, and went his way: thus Esau despised his birthright.

Commentary: Two Nations in One Womb

Abraham Gathered to His People Jacob Have I Loved, Esau Have I Hated The Spiritual and the Carnal A Birthright Despised
Genesis 25 closes out Abraham's own story and opens the story of the next generation, and does it by pairing two very different scenes: a patriarch's quiet death in old age, and a struggle between twins that Brother Branham read as one of the clearest statements of election in all of Scripture.

Abraham Gathered to His People

Verses 1 through 11 record Abraham's later family through Keturah, his careful provision for Isaac above the other sons, and his death at a hundred seventy-five, buried by both Isaac and Ishmael together in the cave of Machpelah beside Sarah. No sourced Branham commentary specifically addressing Keturah's sons or the burial scene itself — notably, Isaac and Ishmael standing together at their father's grave despite the separation of chapter 21 — turned up in the corpus searched for this study. Verses 12 through 18, Ishmael's own genealogy and death, likewise did not turn up direct commentary beyond what was already covered in the Genesis 16 and 21 studies of the wider Ishmael narrative.

Jacob Have I Loved, Esau Have I Hated

Verses 19 through 26 turn to Isaac and Rebekah's own long wait for children, the twins struggling together even in the womb, and the LORD's answer to Rebekah's inquiry naming the two nations that would come from them before either child had done anything at all. Brother Branham preached entire sermons from this exact scene as his central text for the doctrine of election, reading it directly through Paul's own use of it in Romans 9.
“
“For the children being not yet born, neither having done any good or evil, that the purpose of God according to election might stand, not of works, but of him that calleth; it was said unto her, The elder shall serve the younger. As it is written, Jacob have I loved, and Esau have I hated.”
— William Branham, quoting Romans 9:11–13, “Hearing, Recognizing, Acting on the Word of God” (60-0221, para. 7)
He pressed the point that this could not be explained by upbringing or environment, since both boys came from the same believing, consecrated parents.
“
“How could such a thing come through that one mother, by one father? One, a very evil person; and another, very good person…No matter how good your father and mother was, how nice they was, how much Christians your father and mother was, it still lays to you as an individual, your standing before God.”
— William Branham, “Hearing, Recognizing, Acting on the Word of God” (60-0221, paras. 10, 15)

The Spiritual and the Carnal

Verses 27 and 28 sketch the two brothers' opposite natures: Esau the skilled outdoorsman, Jacob the quiet man of the tents. Brother Branham read this contrast not as one brother being religious and the other irreligious, but as two different kinds of religious response — one carnal, one spiritual — a distinction he applied well beyond this one family.
“
“These two boys, if we'll notice their nature, one of them was a spiritual man, that was Jacob. And the carnal man was Esau. But they were both religious. And that same thing has took place all down through the age, the carnal and the spiritual…No one could say they wasn't both religious. They were. They didn't say that one served an 'idol' and the other one 'God.' They were both servants of God.”
— William Branham, “Hearing, Recognizing, Acting on the Word of God” (60-0221, paras. 17, 20)
Type & Shadow — The Law of Contrast
Brother Branham described a "law of contrast" he saw running through Scripture from Eden forward — the tree of life and the tree of death standing in the same garden, and here, two boys from the same womb representing two ongoing spiritual conditions that would never fully separate from one another this side of eternity. He read Esau and Jacob as standing for the carnal, natural religious impulse and the spiritual, called impulse respectively, present together in every generation of professing believers, and read the struggle in Rebekah's womb as evidence that the division between them was fixed by God's own calling rather than decided later by each man's choices.

A Birthright Despised

Verses 29 through 34 record the famous exchange itself: Esau, faint from the field, trading his birthright to Jacob for a bowl of lentil stew. Brother Branham treated this scene as one of his standard illustrations for what it means to trade something of eternal weight for something of the moment, and applied it repeatedly to believers who abandon the deeper things of the Spirit for denominational comfort or worldly ease.
“
“There's one thing Jacob wanted, that was the birthright. Regardless of what come, how he had to get it, what level he had to come on, that birthright was all he cared for. And Esau, the Bible said, despised his birthright…Now, what is a birthright? It's the right. This Holy Spirit is your Birthright; that's your Birthright. That's your God given right.”
— William Branham, “Questions and Answers” (59-1223, paras. 61–62)
He was equally direct about the cost of taking that spiritual reality lightly.
“
“How a man, to hold up a little church doctrine somewhere, because their organization wouldn't agree with It, selling your birthrights for a mess of pottage, Esau, you miserable hypocrite! That's right. The selling of your birthrights for a mess of pottage, for a mess of denomination, a mess of organization that God hates.”
— William Branham, “The Ephesian Church Age” (60-1205, para. 162)

Scripture Cross-References

Romans 9:10–13 — Paul's own use of this chapter as the classic text for election  •  Malachi 1:2–3 — the original prophetic statement, "Jacob have I loved, but Esau have I hated"  •  Hebrews 12:16–17 — Esau, the "profane person," who sold his birthright and later found no place of repentance  •  Genesis 27 — the blessing itself, taken through deception in the chapter that follows  •  Genesis 36:1 — Esau identified as the father of Edom  •  1 Chronicles 1:29–31 — Ishmael's twelve princes listed again, generations later
