Why 40 Authors Across 15 Centuries Never Contradicted Each Other

Try an experiment. Pick forty people at random — never let them meet, never let them coordinate, spread them across fifteen centuries and three continents so none of them could possibly compare notes. Have each one write on the same handful of enormous, controversial questions: where the universe came from, what's wrong with the human race, what a just and merciful God would actually do about it. Now staple all forty manuscripts together and see what you get.

By every reasonable expectation, you get a mess. A pile of contradictions. Forty different answers to "why are we here" that agree on almost nothing.

That experiment already happened, and the result is sitting on your shelf.

The Numbers Are Not an Exaggeration

Scripture's sixty-six books were written by roughly forty different authors over about fifteen centuries — from Moses, writing sometime in the second millennium BC, to John, writing Revelation near the close of the first century AD. Those authors wrote in three languages (Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek), on three continents (Asia, Africa, and Europe), and came from occupations that shared almost nothing in common: a shepherd-turned-prince who became a lawgiver, a tax collector, a fisherman, a physician, a king, a herdsman, a cupbearer to a foreign emperor, a rabbi who spent years in a Roman prison. Most of them never met each other. Many were separated by so many centuries that meeting was never even a possibility.

What "No Contradiction" Actually Requires

It's worth being precise about what the claim is, because it's easy to state it too big or too small. Scripture's writers disagree on emphasis constantly — the four Gospels alone report the same events from four different vantage points, in different order, with different details highlighted. That's not what's being claimed here. The claim is narrower and, honestly, more remarkable: across every controversial subject a forty-person, fifteen-century writing team would be expected to split on — the nature of God, the origin and problem of humanity, the shape of judgment and mercy — the answer doesn't fracture. It develops. Genesis opens with a garden and a closed gate; Revelation closes with a city and an open one. The New Testament doesn't contradict Genesis 3's promise of a coming deliverer — it names Him.

Why This Is the Argument You Can Test Yourself

This is the kind of evidence 777 Reasons the Bible Can Be Trusted was written to lay out in full — not because any single piece of it is a knockdown proof by itself, but because the cumulative case is exactly the kind a careful reader can verify with their own eyes, one cross-reference at a time. Assign ten strangers today the same weighty questions, give them a week, and compare their essays. See how far unity gets you. Then compare that to a book with four times the writers and thirty times the years, and ask what actually explains the difference.
