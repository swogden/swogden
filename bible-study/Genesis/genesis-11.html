Genesis

Chapter Eleven

King James Version
And the whole earth was of one language, and of one speech. 2And it came to pass, as they journeyed from the east, that they found a plain in the land of Shinar; and they dwelt there. 3And they said one to another, Go to, let us make brick, and burn them thoroughly. And they had brick for stone, and slime had they for morter. 4And they said, Go to, let us build us a city and a tower, whose top may reach unto heaven; and let us make us a name, lest we be scattered abroad upon the face of the whole earth.

5And the LORD came down to see the city and the tower, which the children of men builded. 6And the LORD said, Behold, the people is one, and they have all one language; and this they begin to do: and now nothing will be restrained from them, which they have imagined to do. 7Go to, let us go down, and there confound their language, that they may not understand one another's speech. 8So the LORD scattered them abroad from thence upon the face of all the earth: and they left off to build the city. 9Therefore is the name of it called Babel; because the LORD did there confound the language of all the earth: and from thence did the LORD scatter them abroad upon the face of all the earth.

10These are the generations of Shem: Shem was an hundred years old, and begat Arphaxad two years after the flood: 11And Shem lived after he begat Arphaxad five hundred years, and begat sons and daughters. 12And Arphaxad lived five and thirty years, and begat Salah: 13And Arphaxad lived after he begat Salah four hundred and three years, and begat sons and daughters. 14And Salah lived thirty years, and begat Eber: 15And Salah lived after he begat Eber four hundred and three years, and begat sons and daughters.

16And Eber lived four and thirty years, and begat Peleg: 17And Eber lived after he begat Peleg four hundred and thirty years, and begat sons and daughters. 18And Peleg lived thirty years, and begat Reu: 19And Peleg lived after he begat Reu two hundred and nine years, and begat sons and daughters. 20And Reu lived two and thirty years, and begat Serug: 21And Reu lived after he begat Serug two hundred and seven years, and begat sons and daughters.

22And Serug lived thirty years, and begat Nahor: 23And Serug lived after he begat Nahor two hundred years, and begat sons and daughters. 24And Nahor lived nine and twenty years, and begat Terah: 25And Nahor lived after he begat Terah an hundred and nineteen years, and begat sons and daughters. 26And Terah lived seventy years, and begat Abram, Nahor, and Haran.

27Now these are the generations of Terah: Terah begat Abram, Nahor, and Haran; and Haran begat Lot. 28And Haran died before his father Terah in the land of his nativity, in Ur of the Chaldees. 29And Abram and Nahor took them wives: the name of Abram's wife was Sarai; and the name of Nahor's wife, Milcah, the daughter of Haran, the father of Milcah, and the father of Iscah. 30But Sarai was barren; she had no child.

31And Terah took Abram his son, and Lot the son of Haran his son's son, and Sarai his daughter in law, his son Abram's wife; and they went forth with them from Ur of the Chaldees, to go into the land of Canaan; and they came unto Haran, and dwelt there. 32And the days of Terah were two hundred and five years: and Terah died in Haran.

Commentary: A Name Man Made, and a Name God Gave

One Language, One Ambition A Name Instead of Obedience The LORD Came Down From Shem to Terah Out of Ur, Toward a Promise
Genesis 11 falls into two halves that look unrelated until the chapter closes. The first nine verses tell how mankind's unity turned into a monument to itself, and God scattered it. The rest of the chapter is a genealogy that narrows nine generations down to a single childless couple, standing on the edge of a promise the tower-builders never imagined.

One Language, One Ambition

Verses 1 and 2 set the stage plainly: one people, one language, settling on a plain in Shinar — the same territory Genesis 10 already named as the beginning of Nimrod's kingdom. Brother Branham took this shared language as historical fact, not legend, and read the whole episode as the hinge on which the nations divided.
“
“As we come all of one language and one people until the tower of Babel, a confusion. And then their languages went different…And as far as we know they were all one people till that time. And they broke up and scattered in different parts of the world.”
— William Branham, “Questions and Answers 2” (54-0103E, para. 153)

A Name Instead of Obedience

Verses 3 and 4 record the plan itself: brick for stone, tar for mortar, a city and a tower reaching toward heaven, built explicitly so its builders could "make us a name" and avoid being "scattered abroad." That last phrase is worth pausing on — the very thing they built the tower to prevent, God's judgment brought about anyway. Brother Branham described the ambition behind the tower as an attempt to outbuild God's own judgment, a plan to survive whatever wrath might come by human intelligence and engineering rather than by obedience.
“
“It was Nimrod, one day, who said that he would build a tower…that would go beyond the clouds, that, if the wrath of God ever come, he could outsmart Him. With his scientific research, he could place the rocks and the stones in such a way that he could take the people to safety, by his intelligence. But it was brought to naught, by a confusion of tongues, and they couldn't even finish the tower.”
— William Branham, “A Super Sign” (59-1227M, para. 46)

The LORD Came Down

Verses 5 through 9 turn the story around from man's ambition to God's response. God "came down to see" the tower — the same phrase Scripture uses elsewhere for direct divine intervention — and confounded the builders' language so they could no longer work together. The text itself supplies its own explanation of the name: Babel, because there the LORD confounded the language of all the earth. No corpus quotation found for this study comments on verses 5 through 9 directly beyond what is already covered above regarding the tower's motive and its failure, so this commentary limits itself to the plain reading of the text: unity in rebellion was broken by design, not by accident, and the scattering these builders feared became the very thing God used to fill the earth as He had originally commanded Noah's sons to do.
Type & Shadow — A Name Made vs. A Name Given
The builders of Babel wanted to "make us a name." A few chapters later, God changes Abram's own name to Abraham, giving him a name rather than letting him build one. The contrast runs through the rest of Scripture: every name man builds for himself at Babel ends in confusion and scattering, while every name God gives — Abraham, Israel, and ultimately the name above every name in Philippians 2 — ends in a gathered people and a kept promise.

From Shem to Terah

Verses 10 through 26 repeat the pattern of Genesis 5's genealogy in miniature — nine generations from Shem to Terah, each one named, each one given a lifespan in two parts: age at the birth of the next generation's father, and years lived afterward. Unlike Genesis 5, the text never adds up a total for any of these men; it simply hands down the record as it was kept.

Out of Ur, Toward a Promise

The chapter's final verses introduce Terah's household — Abram, Nahor, Haran, and Haran's son Lot — and record Sarai's barrenness before the family ever leaves Ur of the Chaldees. Brother Branham placed this departure in direct continuity with the Babel story just told: Abram's own family line comes down out of the very region, and very likely the very idolatry, that the tower represented.
“
“Abraham the patriarch come down out of Shinar, away from the tower of Babel. He and his father journeyed down into the—the plains. And there, Abraham, at the age of about seventy-five years old, God called Abraham, by election, and sent him out of his own country.”
— William Branham, “God Has a Provided Way” (54-0404E, para. 60)
He made the same point elsewhere, tying Abram's family more directly to the idol-worship that had grown up in Babylon under Nimrod's system.
“
“He was just a Chaldean down there in the city of Ur, come down in the valleys of Shinar, from the tower of Babel, from idol-worship. I think his daddy was an idol-worshipper.”
— William Branham, “Israel and the Church 2” (53-0326, para. 39)
Read this way, Genesis 11 does not simply set two unrelated stories side by side. It moves from the whole earth's failed attempt to make itself a name, straight into the one family God would choose out of that same failed world — a barren, elderly couple who would not reach the land of promise until the next chapter, but whose journey begins here, in Ur, in the shadow of Babel's ruin.
