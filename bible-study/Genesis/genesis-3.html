Genesis

Chapter Three

King James Version
Now the serpent was more subtil than any beast of the field which the LORD God had made. And he said unto the woman, Yea, hath God said, Ye shall not eat of every tree of the garden? 2And the woman said unto the serpent, We may eat of the fruit of the trees of the garden: 3But of the fruit of the tree which is in the midst of the garden, God hath said, Ye shall not eat of it, neither shall ye touch it, lest ye die. 4And the serpent said unto the woman, Ye shall not surely die: 5For God doth know that in the day ye eat thereof, then your eyes shall be opened, and ye shall be as gods, knowing good and evil.

6And when the woman saw that the tree was good for food, and that it was pleasant to the eyes, and a tree to be desired to make one wise, she took of the fruit thereof, and did eat, and gave also unto her husband with her; and he did eat. 7And the eyes of them both were opened, and they knew that they were naked; and they sewed fig leaves together, and made themselves aprons.

8And they heard the voice of the LORD God walking in the garden in the cool of the day: and Adam and his wife hid themselves from the presence of the LORD God amongst the trees of the garden. 9And the LORD God called unto Adam, and said unto him, Where art thou? 10And he said, I heard thy voice in the garden, and I was afraid, because I was naked; and I hid myself. 11And he said, Who told thee that thou wast naked? Hast thou eaten of the tree, whereof I commanded thee that thou shouldest not eat? 12And the man said, The woman whom thou gavest to be with me, she gave me of the tree, and I did eat. 13And the LORD God said unto the woman, What is this that thou hast done? And the woman said, The serpent beguiled me, and I did eat.

14And the LORD God said unto the serpent, Because thou hast done this, thou art cursed above all cattle, and above every beast of the field; upon thy belly shalt thou go, and dust shalt thou eat all the days of thy life: 15And I will put enmity between thee and the woman, and between thy seed and her seed; it shall bruise thy head, and thou shalt bruise his heel.

16Unto the woman he said, I will greatly multiply thy sorrow and thy conception; in sorrow thou shalt bring forth children; and thy desire shall be to thy husband, and he shall rule over thee. 17And unto Adam he said, Because thou hast hearkened unto the voice of thy wife, and hast eaten of the tree, of which I commanded thee, saying, Thou shalt not eat of it: cursed is the ground for thy sake; in sorrow shalt thou eat of it all the days of thy life; 18Thorns also and thistles shall it bring forth to thee; and thou shalt eat the herb of the field; 19In the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat bread, till thou return unto the ground; for out of it wast thou taken: for dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return.

20And Adam called his wife's name Eve; because she was the mother of all living. 21Unto Adam also and to his wife did the LORD God make coats of skins, and clothed them.

22And the LORD God said, Behold, the man is become as one of us, to know good and evil: and now, lest he put forth his hand, and take also of the tree of life, and eat, and live for ever: 23Therefore the LORD God sent him forth from the garden of Eden, to till the ground from whence he was taken. 24So he drove out the man; and he placed at the east of the garden of Eden Cherubims, and a flaming sword which turned every way, to keep the way of the tree of life.

Commentary: Subtlety, Shame, and the First Promise

The Serpent, Not a Reptile Passing the Buck Two Seeds: The First Promise Cain's Paternity Adam Knew His Wife Cain and Abel: Twins The Two Lines to Come Headship: The Second Covenant Fig Leaves vs. Coats of Skins Cherubim & the Flaming Sword
Every fall needs a tempter, and Moses introduces this one carefully: not a monster, not an obviously evil thing, but the single most subtil creature God had made — something Eve had no natural reason to fear or distrust. That word “subtil” is doing real work in verse 1, and Brother Branham built one of his most distinctive and most debated doctrines directly on it.

The Serpent, Not a Reptile

“
“The serpent is that missing person between the chimpanzee and the man…that the serpent was not a reptile. He was most ‘subtil’ of all the beasts of the field.”
— William Branham, “The Serpent's Seed” (58-0928E, para. 145)
He went further into the word itself, and further still into what kind of creature this actually was, before the curse in verse 14 changed its form.
“
“He is smart, crafty, yet he is called the ‘serpent.’ But, remember, he was the smartest thing there was, and the more like the human being than anything else that was on the field; closest to a human being. He was not a reptile. The curse made him a reptile. And…the Bible said he was the most beautiful of all.”
— William Branham, “The Serpent's Seed” (58-0928E, para. 147)
Whatever a reader makes of that, the text itself supports the shape of it: verse 14's curse — “upon thy belly shalt thou go” — is phrased as a change, a sentence handed down, not a description of how the creature already was. Something is lost here that it had before.

Cain's Paternity

This is the point at which Brother Branham's doctrine goes further than the beast's shape or its curse: he taught that the “beguiling” of verse 13 was not merely a lie whispered to Eve, but a literal seduction — that the serpent, in that near-human, upright form, had sexual union with Eve, and that Cain was the son of that union rather than of Adam.
“
“Now remember, Satan's son was Cain…remember that Eve become pregnant by Satan, and in the same day…We got a case of it in Tucson now, that a woman, if she becomes…she lives with two man, she can have two different type of children. We know that. I knowed it in breeding dogs and things, and so forth, if it's right away.”
— William Branham, “Power of Transformation” (65-1031M, para. 126)
He offered the same case again, in more detail, as evidence that two children born close together can have two different fathers — the natural-world proof, as he saw it, standing behind the doctrine.
“
“We got a case there in Tucson now. The—the white woman lived with her husband one morning, and that afternoon she lived with a Negro. And…there was two little boys was born. One of them was a little kinky-headed Negro, and the other one is a—a blond-headed kid, real pretty…So, you see, it's true.”
— William Branham, “Power of Transformation” (65-1031M, para. 134)

“Adam Knew His Wife”

Genesis 4:1 opens, “And Adam knew his wife; and she conceived, and bare Cain.” In a 1962 question-and-answer service, Brother Branham was asked directly how this verse squares with the doctrine, since it reads as though Adam's own knowing of Eve were the cause of the conception. His answer turned on the placement of the conjunction “and.”
“
“In reading the Bible you must know where your conjunctions go. Conjunction is tying your sentence together…So the serpent had beguiled Eve. That's truly. Then after the judgment was set, then Adam knew his wife. And then she conceived and brought forth a son, Cain. You get it? See, the conjunction is just carrying on what Adam did, not what was done before Adam.”
— William Branham, “Questions and Answers” (62-0527, paras. 51, 63)
In other words, he read Genesis 4:1 as recording two separate facts in sequence — Adam later knew his wife, and (already, from what had already happened) she conceived and bare Cain — not as cause and effect. He pressed the same point again a few paragraphs later, tying it directly back to the judgment scene in this chapter.
“
“And God put a curse upon them, and everything like that. And then Adam knew his wife, see, after she was already beguiled and become a mother.”
— William Branham, “Questions and Answers” (62-0527, para. 56)
He then pressed the question a step further: if sin, lying, and murder cannot be traced back to a pure source in Adam, they must have entered through some other father.
“
“But this fellow, where did that pure lying, where did that sin come from? Where did this fellow, Cain, that murderer? And the Bible said that ‘The devil is a murderer.’…It had to come from some resource outside of God. So Cain was the evil one, and his father was Satan; and he brought forth this evil one. And then, course, Adam knew his wife, certainly.”
— William Branham, “Questions and Answers” (62-0527, para. 67)

Cain and Abel: Twins with Two Fathers

On this reading, Cain and Abel were twins — born of the same mother, in the same pregnancy, but of two different fathers.
“
“And so then, you see, then when Adam lived with, she brought forth twins. And one of them was of Satan; and one of them was of Adam, which was of God. Cain and Abel.”
— William Branham, “Power of Transformation” (65-1031M, para. 133)
Brother Branham returned to this “twins” pattern repeatedly across the years, treating Cain and Abel as the first instance of a principle he saw running through the rest of Scripture and church history alike: a true birth on one side answered by a counterfeit birth on the other, in the same moment.
“
“Whenever there is a revival, it always produces twins…When the world was created, it produced twins, two trees. And when Cain and Abel were born, it produced two. When Ishmael and Isaac was born, it produced two. And when Esau and Jacob was born, it produced two. One of them, natural…the other one, Supernatural.”
— William Branham, “Hearing, Recognizing, and Acting on the Word of God” (60-0221, para. 72)
“
“There is twins born in natural man and the spiritual man. It was back in the garden of Eden, Cain and Abel, the same thing, started from there, just keeps coming on down.”
— William Branham, “The Mark of the Beast and the Seal of God 2” (61-0217, para. 80)
“
“It's always been twins all along. There was…Cain and Abel were twins, and Esau and Jacob were twins…Even the Holy Spirit and the antichrist is to be twins, ‘so close that would deceive the very Elected if possible.’”
— William Branham, “Questions and Answers 3” (64-0830M, para. 320)

The Anatomy of the Temptation

The temptation itself is a small masterpiece of misdirection: a question that exaggerates God's command (“hath God said, Ye shall not eat of every tree”), then a flat denial of the stated consequence (“Ye shall not surely die”), then a reframing of disobedience as enlightenment (“your eyes shall be opened, and ye shall be as gods”). Eve eats. Adam eats. And the very first thing their opened eyes show them is not godhood, but their own nakedness.

Passing the Buck

What follows in verses 8 through 13 is one of the most human scenes in all of Scripture: God walking in the garden in the cool of the day, and two people hiding from the sound of Him. When He asks His questions — not because He doesn't know the answers, but because they need to say them — the response is as old as guilt itself: each one points past themselves.
“
“Then they begin to, the army fashion, passing the buck. Said, ‘Well, the woman You give me, done it. She was the one who persuaded me.’”
— William Branham, “The Serpent's Seed” (58-0928E, para. 164)
Eve's own answer gets a sharper edge from the Hebrew word behind it than the English translation lets on.
“
“She said, ‘The serpent beguiled me.’ Do you know what beguile means? Means ‘defiled.’”
— William Branham, “The Serpent's Seed” (58-0928E, para. 166)

Two Seeds: The First Promise

Then the sentence is passed — on the serpent first, then the woman, then the man — and it is inside the very first curse, spoken over the serpent, that the very first promise of redemption in the whole Bible is buried: enmity between two seeds, and a bruised head answered by a bruised heel.
“
“'I will put enmity between thy Seed and the serpent's seed.' What? The serpent seed! She had a Seed, and he had a seed. 'And He shall bruise thy head, and you shall bruise His heel.' And a bruise, there, means ‘to make an Atonement.’”
— William Branham, “The Serpent's Seed” (58-0928E, para. 162)
That reading of verse 15 — a real enmity between two literal seed-lines running through history, resolved only by a bruising that makes atonement — is one of the more demanding pieces of this doctrine, and Brother Branham traced it out as two lines running side by side from Cain and Abel onward: one producing religion, intelligence, and invention without the Spirit, the other carrying the promise itself down to Christ.

The Two Lines to Come

He set the two brothers side by side first — a shrewd, religious, moral man who built his own altar, against a brother who came by revelation rather than by his own effort.
“
“Here comes Cain. Let's watch the natures…He's a shrewd businessman. He tills the fields. Smart, intelligent; religious, very religious…He builds him a church, makes him an offering. Brings an altar, and all…But, by spiritual revelation, Abel knew it was blood. So he brought a lamb, hacked its throat, and it died.”
— William Branham, “The Serpent's Seed” (58-0928E, paras. 170, 172)
From there he traced the righteous line all the way to Christ, and set the serpent's line running in parallel — through Cain, into the land of Nod, and on into a line he identified with the “giants” and the great builders and inventors before the flood.
“
“What come through the line of Abel?…Along came Abel. After Abel come Seth. After Seth come Noah. After Noah come Shem. After Shem come Abraham. After Abraham come Isaac. After Isaac come Jacob. After Jacob come Judah. After Judah come David. After David come Christ, to the perfection.”
— William Branham, “The Serpent's Seed” (58-0928E, para. 182)
“
“Now here comes the seed of the—of the serpent…The seed of the serpent produced Cain. Cain went to the land of Nod, produced giants…They were smart, educated, intelligent people…builders, inventors, scientists; not through the seed of the righteous, but through the seed of Satan, the serpent.”
— William Branham, “The Serpent's Seed” (58-0928E, paras. 193–195)
He was careful, in the same message, to root Cain's murder of Abel back in that same paternity rather than in ordinary human jealousy alone — since Adam himself, he insisted, could not be the source of it.
“
“Could you call God a murderer? And Adam was the son of God. The Bible said, that, ‘Adam was the son of God,’ that pure beginning back there…And that jealousy and envy, and everything, could not come out of that pure stream. It had to come through another place…And it come through Satan, who was a murderer, to begin with.”
— William Branham, “The Serpent's Seed” (58-0928E, paras. 175–176)

Headship: The Second Covenant

Verse 16 marks a change in the relationship between the man and the woman that Brother Branham read as a real, structural shift, not just a hardship.
“
“It was then man was made to rule over the woman, by God's Word. She was no more co-equal with him. She was co-equal in nature, you know; but, when she broke God's Word, God made a man to be a ruler over her. Genesis 3:16…”
— William Branham, “Marriage and Divorce” (65-0221M, para. 97)
Before the fall, he taught, the two stood together as equals in nature under God; the headship of verse 16 is not the original design but a consequence of it, a second covenant laid over the first once the first was broken.

Fig Leaves Versus Coats of Skins

The two coverings in this chapter could not be more different, and Brother Branham treated the contrast between them as the first picture in Scripture of the difference between a man's own religion and God's provided atonement.
“
“Adam and Eve, standing there in the presence of God to hear His Message to them, they had draped about their loins the skin of sheep. The first Message was ever heard, was given over the top of a dead sheepskin, that God had slew and wrapped Adam and Eve in it. After they tried to make their own religion of fig leaves, it wouldn't work. Sheep sacrifice had been instituted for atonement since the very beginning.”
— William Branham, “Why It Had to Be Shepherd” (64-1221, para. 109)
Type & Shadow — Fig Leaves and the Lamb
Fig leaves are what Adam and Eve made for themselves out of the garden's own material, and they failed. Coats of skins are what God made for them, at the cost of a life, and they covered. Every self-made religion since has been a fig leaf; every atonement that has ever actually covered anything has cost a death.

Cherubim and the Flaming Sword

Even the expulsion, guarded by cherubim and a sword of turning flame, reads less like abandonment and more like a Father keeping His children from reaching for a tree that would have made their fallen condition permanent. Adam names his wife Eve — “life,” spoken in the middle of a death sentence. The location of that guard is not incidental; it is where the next chapter's tragedy will actually unfold.
“
“This man, Cain, was a believer. He believed in God. He honored God. He came to worship and he built a altar, built an altar, perhaps east of the garden, 'cause there's where the Cherubim was put with a flaming sword to guard the Tree of Life.”
— William Branham, “I Will Restore, Saith the Lord” (54-0829A, para. 93)
The way back to Eden is barred. The way forward, through a promised Seed, has just been opened — and, on this reading, the two seeds of verse 15 are already growing side by side in Eve's womb before the next chapter even begins.
