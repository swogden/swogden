Genesis

Chapter Two

King James Version
Thus the heavens and the earth were finished, and all the host of them. 2And on the seventh day God ended his work which he had made; and he rested on the seventh day from all his work which he had made. 3And God blessed the seventh day, and sanctified it: because that in it he had rested from all his work which God created and made.

4These are the generations of the heavens and of the earth when they were created, in the day that the LORD God made the earth and the heavens, 5And every plant of the field before it was in the earth, and every herb of the field before it grew: for the LORD God had not caused it to rain upon the earth, and there was not a man to till the ground. 6But there went up a mist from the earth, and watered the whole face of the ground. 7And the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul.

8And the LORD God planted a garden eastward in Eden; and there he put the man whom he had formed. 9And out of the ground made the LORD God to grow every tree that is pleasant to the sight, and good for food; the tree of life also in the midst of the garden, and the tree of knowledge of good and evil. 10And a river went out of Eden to water the garden; and from thence it was parted, and became into four heads. 11The name of the first is Pison: that is it which compasseth the whole land of Havilah, where there is gold; 12And the gold of that land is good: there is bdellium and the onyx stone. 13And the name of the second river is Gihon: the same is it that compasseth the whole land of Ethiopia. 14And the name of the third river is Hiddekel: that is it which goeth toward the east of Assyria. And the fourth river is Euphrates.

15And the LORD God took the man, and put him into the garden of Eden to dress it and to keep it. 16And the LORD God commanded the man, saying, Of every tree of the garden thou mayest freely eat: 17But of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, thou shalt not eat of it: for in the day that thou eatest thereof thou shalt surely die.

18And the LORD God said, It is not good that the man should be alone; I will make him an help meet for him. 19And out of the ground the LORD God formed every beast of the field, and every fowl of the air; and brought them unto Adam to see what he would call them: and whatsoever Adam called every living creature, that was the name thereof. 20And Adam gave names to all cattle, and to the fowl of the air, and to every beast of the field; but for Adam there was not found an help meet for him.

21And the LORD God caused a deep sleep to fall upon Adam, and he slept: and he took one of his ribs, and closed up the flesh instead thereof; 22And the rib, which the LORD God had taken from man, made he a woman, and brought her unto the man. 23And Adam said, This is now bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh: she shall be called Woman, because she was taken out of Man. 24Therefore shall a man leave his father and his mother, and shall cleave unto his wife: and they shall be one flesh. 25And they were both naked, the man and his wife, and were not ashamed.

Commentary: Formed, Placed, and Not Alone

Twofold Creation, Continued Image of Animal Life Naming & Dominion Two Trees: Life & Knowledge Eve, a Type of the Church Not Good to Be Alone
Genesis 1 gave us the whole sweep of creation week from a great distance — days passing like the turning of vast wheels, closing with a single spirit-being, at once male and female in one person, spoken into existence in verses 26 and 27. Genesis 2 does not simply zoom in on that same moment. Brother Branham was direct about this when he was asked how the two chapters relate: they describe two separate creative acts, producing what he called, plainly, two men.
“
“What connection does this Genesis 1:26 have with Genesis 2:7? God created two men. And which was the man…how does it connect up in the Scripture?”
— William Branham, “Questions and Answers on Genesis” (53-0729, para. 14)
The being of Genesis 1:26 was “a dual spirit,” not two spirits side by side, exercising dominion by voice. The man of Genesis 2:7 is a second, later work: a body formed from dust, into which that spirit is placed, becoming “a living soul.” This chapter is that second act, not a closer look at the first one, and it is only later in this chapter, at the taking of the rib, that the single dual spirit is divided into two persons.

Formed of Dust: The Image of Animal Life

Verse 7 is where the chapter actually begins to breathe: God forming man not by a word flung outward, as with light and land, but by hands in the dust, and a breath given mouth to mouth. Brother Branham pictured the moment vividly, and drew a sharp line between this body and the image spoken of in Genesis 1:26.
“
“God formed man out of the dust of the earth, and put this supernatural Spirit…he was laying there. I can see Adam standing…like a tree. God had made him. He was dead as he could be; his toes, like the roots, sticking in the ground. And God said, ‘Let there be,’ or breathed the breath of life into him, and he jumped, came to himself…he became a living soul.”
— William Branham, “Questions and Answers on Genesis” (53-0729, para. 46)
This is where the Message reading of Genesis diverges most sharply from a plain surface reading. The body formed here, Brother Branham taught elsewhere, was not itself made in God's image — that had already happened, spiritually, in Genesis 1:26. The body of dust was framed after the pattern of animal life, and it is the breath God breathes into it that makes the difference between a beast and a man.
“
“In Genesis 2, there was no man to till the soil, and God made man out of the dust of the earth; not in His image, but in the image of animal life. And He put the spirit of man in this animal man here, and he become a living soul…a man is in flesh like a brute, but he's got a soul of God in him, which makes him immortal.”
— William Branham, “Questions and Answers” (54-0515, para. 281)
The two chapters, read together the way Brother Branham read them, describe a man who is already a spirit-being in Genesis 1:26 and only afterward given a physical, animal-patterned body to inhabit in Genesis 2:7 — the “living soul” standing at the join between the two.

The Garden of Two Trees

Then comes the garden itself — planted, not wild; bordered by a river that becomes four; stocked with two named trees standing at its center. Freedom is total here (“of every tree of the garden thou mayest freely eat”) except for one boundary, and that boundary is the first commandment ever given to a man. Brother Branham read the two trees themselves as standing for two ways of knowing God that run through the rest of Scripture.
“
“In the garden of Eden there were two trees, one of them was the knowledge. And the other one the Tree of Life. And when man left the Tree of Life and took the first bite off the tree of knowledge, he separated himself from God…God is not known by knowledge, God's known by faith; by faith, you believe. Not try to figure it out, you just believe.”
— William Branham, “Three Witnesses” (51-0728, para. 16)
Love, from the very beginning, comes wrapped in both liberty and a limit, and the limit stands over a tree named for the very thing it promises: knowledge, reasoned out, in place of the Life that only comes by simply believing what was spoken.

Naming and Dominion

Verse 18 turns the chapter on its only real problem: everything else in creation was pronounced good, but a man alone was not. God's answer is not immediate. First comes a search — every beast and bird paraded before Adam to be named, and in that naming, no small thing happens.
“
“It was, in the beginning, because Adam named them and had power over them. Genesis 1:26, he had dominion over all the earth. He was…made in the image of God and was made to be a…lesser god.”
— William Branham, “The Water Baptism” (61-0120, para. 145)
But naming every living creature only makes the ache plainer: “for Adam there was not found an help meet for him.” Dominion over the animals was never going to answer the need for a companion built like himself.

Eve: A Type of the Church

So God does not make a second creature from the dust the way He made the first. He puts Adam into a deep sleep, and builds the woman from something already living — from Adam's own side. This is the single most important typological verse in the chapter, and Brother Branham built one of his central doctrines directly on it: that what happened to Adam's side prefigures, exactly, what happened at Calvary.
“
“A beautiful type there, all in type, of God taking from the side of Christ, the Bride. See? God opened up Adam's side, taken a rib…And now God opened up the side of Christ, in the…at Calvary, and taken out the Bride. The Church comes through the Blood of Christ, into the Body of Christ.”
— William Branham, “The Cruelty of Sin…” (53-0403, para. 61)
He returned to the same picture a decade later, in far more detail, tying the wound in Adam's side directly to the wound in Christ's, and Eve's origin directly to the Church's.
“
“To restore fallen Eve, as she was a prefigure of the Church. And…as God opened up the side of Adam and took out Eve, by his own flesh and blood…so God did the same thing, taking out of the side of Christ, the Blood and the water. And Christ is the Word, and taking the Word and making up His Church, Eve…The Bride has to be the Word, for He is the Word. She cannot stand on creeds. She cannot stand on denomination…She has to stand alone on the Word, because She is part of It. She was taken from Christ.”
— William Branham, “Christ Is the Mystery of God Revealed” (63-0728, para. 185, 187)
Type & Shadow — Adam's Side and Calvary's Spear
A rib taken from a sleeping, unwounded-in-spirit Adam becomes the pattern for blood and water taken from a pierced, dying Christ: in both cases, a bride formed out of the very substance of the one she belongs to, while he sleeps or while he is given over to death.
“
“Out of Adam's side come a rib that made his bride. And out of the side of Christ came the Blood, water, Spirit, for His Bride.”
— William Branham, “The Law Having a Shadow” (56-0621, para. 122)
Brother Branham pressed the typology further still, back to the twofold creation of chapter one: Adam, before Eve was separated out of him, was “both male and female…spiritually speaking,” and Eve's body was a later “by-product,” but her spirit had been part of Adam from the start.
“
“Notice how that Eve was united with Adam, she become part of him…Did you notice the body part was a by-product, but not the spirit? The body part of the woman was a by-product, after the creation had been finished, God took from the side of Adam a rib and made a woman. But not the spirit, the spirit was part of Adam, for he was both man and woman…spiritually speaking, both masculine and feminish.”
— William Branham, “Oneness” (62-0211, para. 19)

Not Good to Be Alone

Whatever else the chapter is doing typologically, it never stops being a plain account of the first marriage. Brother Branham read the anatomy of it just as literally as the doctrine.
“
“God made Eve from Adam's side. The woman has one more rib today in the anatomy and the makeup than man does, because a rib was taken from Adam's body. Adam had already been made and was living, and was lonesome, and then God said, ‘It's not good that man should live alone.’”
— William Branham, “Questions and Answers on Genesis” (53-0729, para. 96)
Adam's own words when he wakes are the first poetry spoken by a man: “bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh.” Not a stranger, not another creature to be named and catalogued like the animals, but something taken out of himself and given back to him as a companion. The chapter closes on the plainest possible picture of what marriage was before anything went wrong with it: two people, naked, unashamed, and not alone — and, read the other way, a Bridegroom and a Bride, one Word and one Blood between them, with nothing yet to be ashamed of.
