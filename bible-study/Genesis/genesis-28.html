Chapter Twenty-Eight

King James Version
And Isaac called Jacob, and blessed him, and charged him, and said unto him, Thou shalt not take a wife of the daughters of Canaan. 2Arise, go to Padanaram, to the house of Bethuel thy mother's father; and take thee a wife from thence of the daughters of Laban thy mother's brother. 3And God Almighty bless thee, and make thee fruitful, and multiply thee, that thou mayest be a multitude of people; 4And give thee the blessing of Abraham, to thee, and to thy seed with thee; that thou mayest inherit the land wherein thou art a stranger, which God gave unto Abraham. 5And Isaac sent away Jacob: and he went to Padanaram unto Laban, son of Bethuel the Syrian, the brother of Rebekah, Jacob's and Esau's mother.

6When Esau saw that Isaac had blessed Jacob, and sent him away to Padanaram, to take him a wife from thence; and that as he blessed him he gave him a charge, saying, Thou shalt not take a wife of the daughters of Canaan; 7And that Jacob obeyed his father and his mother, and was gone to Padanaram; 8And Esau seeing that the daughters of Canaan pleased not Isaac his father; 9Then went Esau unto Ishmael, and took unto the wives which he had Mahalath the daughter of Ishmael Abraham's son, the sister of Nebajoth, to be his wife.

10And Jacob went out from Beersheba, and went toward Haran. 11And he lighted upon a certain place, and tarried there all night, because the sun was set; and he took of the stones of that place, and put them for his pillows, and lay down in that place to sleep. 12And he dreamed, and behold a ladder set up on the earth, and the top of it reached to heaven: and behold the angels of God ascending and descending on it. 13And, behold, the LORD stood above it, and said, I am the LORD God of Abraham thy father, and the God of Isaac: the land whereon thou liest, to thee will I give it, and to thy seed; 14And thy seed shall be as the dust of the earth, and thou shalt spread abroad to the west, and to the east, and to the north, and to the south: and in thee and in thy seed shall all the families of the earth be blessed. 15And, behold, I am with thee, and will keep thee in all places whither thou goest, and will bring thee again into this land; for I will not leave thee, until I have done that which I have spoken to thee of.

16And Jacob awaked out of his sleep, and he said, Surely the LORD is in this place; and I knew it not. 17And he was afraid, and said, How dreadful is this place! this is none other but the house of God, and this is the gate of heaven. 18And Jacob rose up early in the morning, and took the stone that he had put for his pillows, and set it up for a pillar, and poured oil upon the top of it. 19And he called the name of that place Bethel: but the name of that city was called Luz at the first.

20And Jacob vowed a vow, saying, If God will be with me, and will keep me in this way that I go, and will give me bread to eat, and raiment to put on, 21So that I come again to my father's house in peace; then shall the LORD be my God: 22And this stone, which I have set for a pillar, shall be God's house: and of all that thou shalt give me I will surely give the tenth unto thee.

Commentary: A Fugitive's Pillow, a Promise Kept Anyway

Not Because He Deserved It The Ladder With Two Ends Surely the LORD Is in This Place Jacob's Vow
Genesis 28 opens with Isaac's blessing sending Jacob away to find a wife among his own people, and Esau's own attempt, in verse 9, to please his father by a marriage of his own. But the chapter's center of gravity is the night Jacob spends alone in the open country between Beersheba and Haran — a fugitive with a stone for a pillow, granted a vision of heaven opened that he had done nothing to earn.

A Wanderer, Not Because He Deserved It

Brother Branham returned more than once to the plain fact of Jacob's condition on this night: a man running for his life, alone, exhausted, glancing back for the sound of Esau's horse. He was careful to name exactly why God met him there — not as a reward for Jacob's character, which the previous chapter had just shown to be built on deception, but because of a promise God had already made to Abraham and Isaac, resting in Jacob's own body whether he had earned it or not.
“
“Now, Jacob, as a wanderer, yet in the promise he run for his life. Well, he wouldn't have got killed anyhow. God was with him. And then one day after tired, running through the wilderness and looking back, see if Esau was coming…In the evening the sun was going down, the poor little fellow was tired, so he just rolled some rocks together and laid down for a rest. Notice, he fell into a trance. God was talking to His servant, because in His servant, not because he was righteous, but because God had made a promise…Look the way Jacob got it. But God had made a promise and had to keep it. In the loins of Jacob laid the blessed righteous seed coming forth from there according to the seed Isaac his father, which had been promised though Abraham.”
— William Branham, “The Infallibility of God's Spoken Word” (56-0404, paras. 27–28)
“
“God took Jacob and showed him some of the future, knowing that he was an heir of the promise, not because he deserved it, because God had promised it. Not because that you got a right, not because that something you done, but God's give you that same kind of a promise. For when we are dead in Christ, we take on Abraham's Seed, and are heirs with Abraham in the promise.”
— William Branham, “The Infallibility of God's Spoken Word” (56-0404, para. 29)
This is the same unconditional-covenant doctrine already drawn from Genesis 20 — God's promise resting on His own word rather than on the recipient's conduct — now applied to the very man whose conduct, in the chapter just finished, was least deserving of a vision. Jacob had lied to his father and stolen a blessing meant for his brother; the same night he becomes a fugitive for it, heaven opens over him anyway.
Type & Shadow — The Ladder With Two Ends
Brother Branham read the ladder itself as a picture of the single road between heaven and earth, anchored at both ends rather than resting loosely on either. He applied the image directly to the believer's own standing rather than treating it only as a piece of ancient history.
“
“A ladder has two ends to it. Amen. One's anchored in God; the other one's anchored in Christ. And we're caught between it with a great host of Angels bringing forth ministering spirits, supernatural gifts and things, vindicating we're on our road, marching up…this foundation of this great Holy Ghost road that we're traveling tonight, Jacob's ladder, is hooked onto the Throne of God, and the heart of Jesus Christ and we're in-between it.”
— William Branham, “The Infallibility of God's Spoken Word” (56-0404, paras. 54–55)
Jesus Himself points back to this exact scene in John 1:51, telling Nathanael, "Hereafter ye shall see heaven open, and the angels of God ascending and descending upon the Son of man" — placing Himself, rather than a literal stairway, as the true connection between heaven and earth that Jacob saw only in a dream. No sourced Branham commentary connecting Genesis 28's ladder directly to John 1:51 by that specific verse turned up in the corpus searched for this study, but the identification is Christ's own, made explicitly in the Gospel text, and it stands alongside Brother Branham's reading of the ladder's two ends without needing to be argued for further.

Surely the LORD Is in This Place

Jacob's own words on waking — "Surely the LORD is in this place; and I knew it not" — and his naming of the site Bethel, "the house of God," mark this as the second time in Genesis that a patriarch is caught by surprise in the presence of God at a place that looked, until that moment, like ordinary ground: Abraham at Mamre, and now Jacob asleep in open country with nothing but stones under his head. No sourced Branham commentary specifically addressing Jacob's words in verses 16 and 17, or the anointing of the stone pillar in verse 18, turned up in the corpus searched for this study.

Jacob's Vow

The chapter closes with Jacob's own vow — a conditional promise to make the LORD his God and to tithe, contingent on God's care for him along the way. No sourced Branham commentary specifically addressing the terms of this vow turned up in the corpus searched for this study. It is worth noting plainly, without commentary supplied to smooth it over, that Jacob's language here is bargaining rather than simple worship — "if God will be with me…then shall the LORD be my God" — the same calculating instinct already on display in his dealings with Esau and Isaac, now turned toward God directly.

Scripture Cross-References

Genesis 12:1–3 — the original Abrahamic promise, renewed here to Jacob word for word  •  Genesis 26:24 — the same promise renewed to Isaac at Beersheba  •  Genesis 18:1–2 — Abraham's own surprise encounter with God at Mamre  •  John 1:51 — Jesus identifying Himself as the true ladder between heaven and earth  •  Romans 5:8 — "while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us," the pattern of grace preceding worthiness  •  Genesis 35:1–15 — Jacob's return to Bethel years later, to fulfill this vow
