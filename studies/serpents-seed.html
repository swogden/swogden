Sermon Study · 58-0928E

The Serpent's Seed

William Branham · Branham Tabernacle, Jeffersonville, Indiana · September 28, 1958

♫ Listen to or read the full sermon at Voice Of God Recordings →

No sermon in this collection is likely to generate more pushback than this one, so it's worth saying plainly up front what it is: Branham's own explanation for the "missing link" science has never found between animal and human life, built into a doctrine about two distinct spiritual bloodlines running through the whole of human history. It's a minority reading even among people otherwise sympathetic to his ministry, and I'm presenting it here the way I've tried to present everything else in this project — accurately, in his own framing, without either softening it or piling on. Read it, weigh it, and draw your own conclusions.

The Claim, Plainly Stated

Branham's argument runs like this: the serpent of Genesis 3 was not a snake in the modern sense. Before its curse, he says, it was an upright, highly intelligent, strikingly beautiful creature — closer to human than any animal, close enough that its "blood" could mix with Eve's, which is why (in his reasoning) no animal-human hybrid has ever been produced, but this one union was possible. Satan indwelt that creature, seduced Eve, and Cain was the biological result — making Cain, in Branham's phrase, "the son of Satan." Abel, meanwhile, was Adam's own son. From there, Genesis 3:15's "enmity between thy seed and her Seed" becomes, in this reading, a literal statement about two competing bloodlines running in parallel through the rest of Scripture and human history, rather than a purely symbolic statement about sin and the ultimate victory of Christ over the devil.

The Case He Builds For It

Branham leans hard on the word "subtil" in Genesis 3:1, arguing from Hebrew that it implies craft and a kind of true knowledge rather than mere reptilian cunning — and from there argues that Eve's realization of her nakedness "had to come sexually," since eating literal fruit doesn't produce that kind of shame. He also points to the impossibility of cross-breeding humans with any animal as evidence that whatever produced Cain had to be something nearer to human than any known species, and reads the specific wording of the curse — the serpent losing its legs, going on its belly — as evidence that it once stood upright. It's a sermon built the way a lawyer builds a case: pull a philological thread here, a biological observation there, and assemble them into a single narrative.

A Detail Worth Weighing Fairly

In the middle of this sermon — one that a critic might expect to lean toward some kind of hierarchy of bloodlines or peoples — Branham does the opposite. He states directly that "God of one blood made all men," rejects any notion that a Black man, a Chinese man, or a white man could rightly say "I have nothing to do with you" to one another, and recounts pushing back against someone in Africa who called the people there "nothing but animals." Whatever a reader makes of the serpent's-seed doctrine itself, it isn't, in this sermon at least, deployed toward racial hierarchy — if anything, this passage argues explicitly against it. That's worth including for anyone who's only heard the doctrine described secondhand.

What This Doctrine Is Doing Theologically

Whatever you make of the biology, it's worth noticing what work this teaching does inside Branham's larger framework: it gives him an explanation for why some people, in his view, never seem to respond to the Gospel no matter how it's presented — not because of a fixed group identity, but because of an inherited spiritual nature traced all the way back to two different fathers in Eden. It also lets him read Cain as a type: religious, moral, hardworking, and still fundamentally on the wrong side of the divide, which becomes a recurring warning throughout his preaching against relying on religious performance instead of genuine spiritual birth.

For Reflection

  • Branham builds this doctrine from a chain of inferences — a word study, a biological observation, a reading of a curse. Where in your own thinking do you draw the line between a compelling inference and a claim that needs direct textual support?
  • This sermon includes an explicit, unprompted argument against racial hierarchy in the middle of a doctrine that critics sometimes associate with exactly that. How should a passage like that shape how fairly we represent a controversial teaching?
  • Cain is presented here as religious, hardworking, and still spiritually lost. Whether or not you accept the serpent's-seed framework, does that particular warning — that religious performance isn't the same as genuine standing with God — stand on its own?
