Genesis

Chapter Twenty-Three

King James Version
And Sarah was an hundred and seven and twenty years old: these were the years of the life of Sarah. 2And Sarah died in Kirjatharba; the same is Hebron in the land of Canaan: and Abraham came to mourn for Sarah, and to weep for her.

3And Abraham stood up from before his dead, and spake unto the sons of Heth, saying, 4I am a stranger and a sojourner with you: give me a possession of a buryingplace with you, that I may bury my dead out of my sight. 5And the children of Heth answered Abraham, saying unto him, 6Hear us, my lord: thou art a mighty prince among us: in the choice of our sepulchres bury thy dead; none of us shall withhold from thee his sepulchre, but that thou mayest bury thy dead.

7And Abraham stood up, and bowed himself to the people of the land, even to the children of Heth. 8And he communed with them, saying, If it be your mind that I should bury my dead out of my sight; hear me, and intreat for me to Ephron the son of Zohar, 9That he may give me the cave of Machpelah, which he hath, which is in the end of his field; for as much money as it is worth he shall give it me for a possession of a buryingplace amongst you.

10And Ephron dwelt among the children of Heth: and Ephron the Hittite answered Abraham in the audience of the children of Heth, even of all that went in at the gate of his city, saying, 11Nay, my lord, hear me: the field give I thee, and the cave that is therein, I give it thee; in the presence of the sons of my people give I it thee: bury thy dead. 12And Abraham bowed down himself before the people of the land. 13And he spake unto Ephron in the audience of the people of the land, saying, But if thou wilt give it, I pray thee, hear me: I will give thee money for the field; take it of me, and I will bury my dead there.

14And Ephron answered Abraham, saying unto him, 15My lord, hearken unto me: the land is worth four hundred shekels of silver; what is that betwixt me and thee? bury therefore thy dead. 16And Abraham hearkened unto Ephron; and Abraham weighed to Ephron the silver, which he had named in the audience of the sons of Heth, four hundred shekels of silver, current money with the merchant.

17And the field of Ephron, which was in Machpelah, which was before Mamre, the field, and the cave which was therein, and all the trees that were in the field, that were in all the borders round about, were made sure 18Unto Abraham for a possession in the presence of the children of Heth, before all that went in at the gate of his city. 19And after this, Abraham buried Sarah his wife in the cave of the field of Machpelah before Mamre: the same is Hebron in the land of Canaan.

20And the field, and the cave that is therein, were made sure unto Abraham for a possession of a buryingplace by the sons of Heth.

Study: The First Ground He Ever Owned

After the cosmic intensity of Moriah, this chapter turns entirely personal and quiet. Sarah dies at a hundred and twenty-seven, at Hebron, and Abraham's grief is stated in the plainest possible terms: he "came to mourn for Sarah, and to weep for her." No vision, no covenant, no test — just an old man burying his wife.
What follows is unusually detailed for Genesis, which normally moves fast and summarizes conversations in a line or two. Here, an entire real-estate negotiation is recorded almost word for word: Abraham's request, the Hittites' generous offer of any tomb he likes, his insistence on dealing directly with Ephron, Ephron's offer of the field and cave as an outright gift, and Abraham's refusal to accept it for free. He wants to pay full price, named publicly, weighed out in front of witnesses at the city gate.
That insistence matters more than it might first appear. A field given as a favor could always be disputed or reclaimed later by someone else's descendants. A field bought at full price, weighed out in named currency, in the hearing of everyone who passed through the city gate, becomes something no one can credibly take back. Abraham isn't just being polite — he's making sure this transaction can never be undone.
It's worth pausing on what this moment actually represents in the larger story. Genesis 12 promised Abraham this entire land. Genesis 15 and 17 renewed that promise formally, twice, with covenant and oath. Genesis 22 confirmed it again after the hardest test imaginable. And through all of it, Abraham has owned, legally and permanently, not one square foot of Canaan — until this chapter, over a grave.
One reading ties that choice of burial ground to something much larger than sentiment, following the same insistence forward through the rest of the patriarchs.
“
“When Abraham died, he slept with Sarah, the same place... And when Isaac begot Jacob, and Jacob died down in Egypt. But before he died, he made his prophet son, Joseph... swore by God that he'd not bury him down in Egypt... 'Take me back up into Canaan. Bury me up there with my father Abraham, with Isaac.' ...God hadn't promised a resurrection down there in Egypt, but they knew that the resurrection was going to be in the promised land.”
— William Branham, “Redeemer, Redemption” (55-1119)
Whatever a reader makes of that reasoning, the pattern in the text itself is real: Sarah, Abraham, Isaac, Rebekah, Leah, and Jacob are all eventually buried in this same cave at Machpelah, generations of a family insisting on the same small plot of ground rather than wherever they happened to die. What began as one grieving man's stubborn refusal to accept a favor becomes, over the rest of Genesis, the family's one fixed, undeniable claim to the land they were promised but wouldn't live to see given to their descendants.
The chapter closes with something close to a legal seal: "the field, and the cave that is therein, were made sure unto Abraham... by the sons of Heth." Not a word from heaven. Not a sign in the sky. Just a deed, witnessed and final — the first piece of ground in the whole story that actually, legally belongs to the man it was promised to.
