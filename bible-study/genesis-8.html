Genesis

Chapter Eight

King James Version
And God remembered Noah, and every living thing, and all the cattle that was with him in the ark: and God made a wind to pass over the earth, and the waters asswaged; 2The fountains also of the deep and the windows of heaven were stopped, and the rain from heaven was restrained; 3And the waters returned from off the earth continually: and after the end of the hundred and fifty days the waters were abated. 4And the ark rested in the seventh month, on the seventeenth day of the month, upon the mountains of Ararat. 5And the waters decreased continually until the tenth month: in the tenth month, on the first day of the month, were the tops of the mountains seen.

6And it came to pass at the end of forty days, that Noah opened the window of the ark which he had made: 7And he sent forth a raven, which went forth to and fro, until the waters were dried up from off the earth. 8Also he sent forth a dove from him, to see if the waters were abated from off the face of the ground; 9But the dove found no rest for the sole of her foot, and she returned unto him into the ark, for the waters were on the face of the whole earth: then he put forth his hand, and took her, and pulled her in unto him into the ark. 10And he stayed yet other seven days; and again he sent forth the dove out of the ark; 11And the dove came in to him in the evening; and, lo, in her mouth was an olive leaf pluckt off: so Noah knew that the waters were abated from off the earth. 12And he stayed yet other seven days; and sent forth the dove; which returned not again unto him any more.

13And it came to pass in the six hundredth and first year, in the first month, the first day of the month, the waters were dried up from off the earth: and Noah removed the covering of the ark, and looked, and, behold, the face of the ground was dry. 14And in the second month, on the seven and twentieth day of the month, was the earth dried.

15And God spake unto Noah, saying, 16Go forth of the ark, thou, and thy wife, and thy sons, and thy sons' wives with thee. 17Bring forth with thee every living thing that is with thee, of all flesh, both of fowl, and of cattle, and of every creeping thing that creepeth upon the earth; that they may breed abundantly in the earth, and be fruitful, and multiply upon the earth. 18And Noah went forth, and his sons, and his wife, and his sons' wives with him: 19Every beast, every creeping thing, and every fowl, and whatsoever creepeth upon the earth, after their kinds, went forth out of the ark.

20And Noah builded an altar unto the LORD; and took of every clean beast, and of every clean fowl, and offered burnt offerings on the altar. 21And the LORD smelled a sweet savour; and the LORD said in his heart, I will not again curse the ground any more for man's sake; for the imagination of man's heart is evil from his youth; neither will I again smite any more every thing living, as I have done. 22While the earth remaineth, seedtime and harvest, and cold and heat, and summer and winter, and day and night shall not cease.

Study: The Dove That Found No Rest

After a chapter that ended on nothing but water and totality, Genesis 8 opens with four quiet words that turn the whole story: "And God remembered Noah." Nothing about Noah's situation has changed yet in that instant — he's still sealed inside a wooden box somewhere on an ocean with no land in sight — but the narrative itself pivots on being remembered rather than forgotten.
“
“In the time that when God was going to destroy all the wickedness off of the face of the earth, God remembered Noah. And Noah remembers how he escaped the wrath of God, by God's mercy... God had Noah inside the ark. Now, if that ain't a wonderful place to remember Him by, yeah, yes, to be inside the ark, safely secured in the Presence of God.”
— William Branham, “Remembering The Lord” (63-0122)
What follows is a slow, deliberate waiting — months measured out precisely, the ark grounded on Ararat long before there's anywhere to actually go. Noah's two test birds in verses 6 through 12 aren't a random detail; the contrast between them says something about the nature of each creature sent out into a ruined world.
“
“So he went and got an untrusted bird, treacherous, and he sent him out. He was a crow, and that crow never did return, because he found pleasure out there, outside the ark of God. He flew from one old dead body to another, eating the carcasses... So after several days he tried again... he turned a dove loose. And this dove was of a different nature... It was not a vulture, neither is it a scavenger, it can only eat the clean pure things. And it was so satisfied because it couldn't find nothing else to put the soles of his feet on, it returned back to the ark... [Later] she picked off a olive leaf off of a tree, and flew back... God used a dove for a sign. She come back saying that the flood is over.”
— William Branham, “On the Wings of a Snow-White Dove” (65-1128E)
That same sermon draws the dove forward into the New Testament — the same bird, the same sign, resting on Christ at His baptism in Matthew 3:16. A raven can live indefinitely on a world full of death and find it perfectly agreeable. A dove, by its very nature, can find nowhere to land in that same world until something has actually been made clean. The difference between the two birds is the difference between a nature that adapts to corruption and a nature that simply cannot rest in it.
Even once dry ground is confirmed, Noah doesn't leave the ark on his own initiative. Verses 15 through 17 have God giving the command to come out in exactly the same detailed, itemized way He gave the command to go in back in chapter 7. Obedience opened the door of this story, and obedience closes it — nothing here is left to Noah simply deciding the time felt right.
And the very first thing Noah does on solid ground is not settle, farm, or rest. He builds an altar. Of everything he could have done first, after over a year confined to a boat, worship comes before anything else — and it's that offering, not the landing itself, that God responds to: a promise that the ground will never again be cursed this way, and that seedtime and harvest, summer and winter, day and night, will not cease as long as the earth remains. It's worth noting plainly that the sign confirming that promise — the rainbow — doesn't actually appear until the next chapter. What we see here is the promise being spoken; what it looks like sealed is Genesis 9's subject, not this one.
