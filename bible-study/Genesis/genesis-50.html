Genesis

Chapter Fifty

King James Version
And Joseph fell upon his father's face, and wept upon him, and kissed him. 2And Joseph commanded his servants the physicians to embalm his father: and the physicians embalmed Israel. 3And forty days were fulfilled for him; for so are fulfilled the days of those which are embalmed: and the Egyptians mourned for him threescore and ten days.

4And when the days of his mourning were past, Joseph spake unto the house of Pharaoh, saying, If now I have found grace in your eyes, speak, I pray you, in the ears of Pharaoh, saying, 5My father made me swear, saying, Lo, I die: in my grave which I have digged for me in the land of Canaan, there shalt thou bury me. Now therefore let me go up, I pray thee, and bury my father, and I will come again. 6And Pharaoh said, Go up, and bury thy father, according as he made thee swear.

7And Joseph went up to bury his father: and with him went up all the servants of Pharaoh, the elders of his house, and all the elders of the land of Egypt, 8And all the house of Joseph, and his brethren, and his father's house: only their little ones, and their flocks, and their herds, they left in the land of Goshen. 9And there went up with him both chariots and horsemen: and it was a very great company.

10And they came to the threshingfloor of Atad, which is beyond Jordan, and there they mourned with a great and very sore lamentation: and he made a mourning for his father seven days. 11And when the inhabitants of the land, the Canaanites, saw the mourning in the floor of Atad, they said, This is a grievous mourning to the Egyptians: wherefore the name of it was called Abelmizraim, which is beyond Jordan.

12And his sons did unto him according as he commanded them: 13For his sons carried him into the land of Canaan, and buried him in the cave of the field of Machpelah, which Abraham bought with the field for a possession of a buryingplace of Ephron the Hittite, before Mamre. 14And Joseph returned into Egypt, he, and his brethren, and all that went up with him to bury his father, after he had buried his father.

15And when Joseph's brethren saw that their father was dead, they said, Joseph will peradventure hate us, and will certainly requite us all the evil which we did unto him. 16And they sent a messenger unto Joseph, saying, Thy father did command before he died, saying, 17So shall ye say unto Joseph, Forgive, I pray thee now, the trespass of thy brethren, and their sin; for they did unto thee evil: and now, we pray thee, forgive the trespass of the servants of the God of thy father. And Joseph wept when they spake unto him.

18And his brethren also went and fell down before his face; and they said, Behold, we be thy servants. 19And Joseph said unto them, Fear not: for am I in the place of God? 20But as for you, ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good, to bring to pass, as it is this day, to save much people alive. 21Now therefore fear ye not: I will nourish you, and your little ones. And he comforted them, and spake kindly unto them.

22And Joseph dwelt in Egypt, he, and his father's house: and Joseph lived an hundred and ten years. 23And Joseph saw Ephraim's children of the third generation: the children also of Machir the son of Manasseh were brought up upon Joseph's knees.

24And Joseph said unto his brethren, I die: and God will visit you, and bring you out of this land unto the land which he sware to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob. 25And Joseph took an oath of the children of Israel, saying, God will surely visit you, and ye shall carry up my bones from hence.

26So Joseph died, being an hundred and ten years old: and they embalmed him, and put him in a coffin in Egypt.

Study: God Meant It Unto Good

Joseph's grief this time is immediate and entirely physical — falling on his father's face, weeping, kissing him. There's no need to leave the room to compose himself, the way there was back in chapters 43 and 45. What follows is practical and enormous in scale: forty days of Egyptian embalming, seventy full days of national mourning for a foreign shepherd's father — an honor that says as much about how Egypt regards Joseph as it does about Jacob himself.
The funeral procession that carries Jacob home is described almost as a small army — "chariots and horsemen... a very great company," Egyptian officials and elders traveling alongside Joseph's own family. It's large and visibly grief-stricken enough that the Canaanite locals watching from a distance rename the place entirely: Abelmizraim, "the mourning of Egypt." A foreign nation's sorrow becomes a permanent mark on the map of Canaan.
Jacob's burial fulfills, for the third time across three chapters now, the same insistence this family has carried since Abraham first bought that plot of ground outright in chapter 23: Machpelah, with his fathers, nowhere else.
One reading treats that coffin as something far more than a burial detail — a physical object that shaped four hundred years of Israel's life in slavery.
“
“Think of the beaten backs of those poor old Hebrews, so tormented by the Egyptian persecutors. But every time they'd look over and see that little casket there, the bones of Joseph, they'd say, 'Some glorious day we're going out of here. For the prophet has promised us that those bones, before they are deteriorated, we'll go to Palestine and we'll go home.' What a perfect type Joseph was there, of Jesus.”
— William Branham, “The Angel of the Covenant” (54-0301)
That same reading recurs elsewhere in strikingly consistent terms — a coffin serving as a standing memorial, carried by an enslaved people who had no other visible reason left to hope, until Moses himself finally gathered those bones and carried them home to be buried in the same ground as Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.
A second reading connects Joseph's specific insistence on Canaan — refusing even the honor of a permanent Egyptian burial — to something shared by every patriarch in this book.
“
“'Take my bones up there, and bury them... up in the promised land, up in Canaan land, out of Egypt.' So when Moses come in, another inspired prophet, he taken the bones of Joseph, and took them up and buried them in the same field... There was Something in him. No matter what the rest of the world thought, what they done, that had nothing to do with Joseph; that had nothing to do with Abraham, with Isaac, with Jacob, with all the rest of them. Something pressed to that promised land.”
— William Branham, “My Redeemer Liveth” (55-0410S)
Whatever a reader makes of that framing, the plain fact remains striking on its own: every single patriarch in this book, given every reason and every honor Egypt could offer to stay, insists instead on a burial plot back in Canaan — the same one Abraham first bought outright in chapter 23, still holding the whole family's confidence more than a hundred and fifty years later.
Once Jacob is gone, the brothers' fear surfaces immediately and tells its own story: they assume Joseph's kindness over the last seventeen years was only ever extended for their father's sake, and that nothing now stands between them and the reckoning they still believe they deserve. They send word claiming Jacob commanded, before he died, that Joseph forgive them. The text never confirms whether that message is genuine or something the brothers invented out of desperation — a small, telling detail of how little they still trust the reconciliation, even after nearly two decades of peace.
Joseph's answer is the chapter's real center, and arguably the theological center of the entire book of Genesis. "Am I in the place of God?" isn't a dodge — it's a genuine refusal to sit in judgment over them, a role he's careful not to claim for himself. What he says next is worth reading exactly as written, because it holds two things together without collapsing either one: "ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good, to bring to pass, as it is this day, to save much people alive." He doesn't deny what they intended. He names it plainly — real evil, real intent. But he refuses to let their intent be the only thing that determined the outcome. Both truths stand at once: what they meant, and what God did with it.
A note on sourcing: despite how often verse 20 gets quoted as one of the most important lines in Genesis, I searched hard and found nothing addressing it directly in the library. The bones of Joseph, further down in this chapter, turned out to be a different story entirely — well covered, once I widened the search terms.
Joseph lives long enough to see his own great-great-grandchildren, and when death finally comes at a hundred and ten, his last instructions look entirely forward rather than back. Unlike his father, he doesn't ask to be carried to Canaan immediately. He makes his brothers swear something stranger and more patient: that when God eventually visits them — not now, but someday — they will carry his bones up out of Egypt with them. It's a statement of faith in an exodus he will never live to see, one that Scripture confirms actually happened, centuries later, in Exodus 13:19 and Joshua 24:32.
Genesis ends there: not with a resolved ending, but with a promise still in motion, a coffin in Egypt waiting on a future none of the people burying him will live to witness. Fifty chapters, one long family's whole beginning, closing on nothing more or less than an old man's confidence that God's word doesn't end where the story does.
