One Verse, One Minute: 1 Kings 19:12

1 Kings 19:12, King James Version:

"And after the earthquake a fire; but the LORD was not in the fire: and after the fire a still small voice."

That phrase — "still small voice" — is one of the most quoted lines in the Old Testament. It's also, depending on which translation you're holding, not quite what the Hebrew actually says.

What the Hebrew Says

The phrase is qol demamah daqqah. Broken down: qol means "voice" or "sound." Demamah means something closer to "silence" or "stillness" — it's related to a root that means "to be still" or "to cease." Daqqah means "thin" or "fine," the same word used elsewhere in scripture for fine dust or thin flakes.

So a more literal rendering lands closer to "a sound of thin silence" or "a voice, a thin silence" — which is a genuinely strange, almost self-contradictory phrase in the original language too. It's not describing a gentle whisper the way "still small voice" tends to sound in English. It's describing something closer to the sound silence itself makes — a paradox, on purpose.

Why Translators Disagree

This is why you'll see real variety across English Bibles: the KJV's "still small voice," the NIV's "a gentle whisper," the NRSV's "a sound of sheer silence," the NASB's "a sound of a gentle blowing." None of these are wrong exactly — they're all wrestling with a Hebrew phrase that resists a clean English equivalent.

What every version agrees on, though, is the contrast: whatever this was, it was categorically different from the wind, the earthquake, and the fire that came before it. The text goes out of its way, three separate times, to tell us the Lord was not in those. Then this — whisper, silence, sound, whatever we call it in English — is where Elijah finally covers his face.

Sixty seconds well spent, if it means never reading "still small voice" as just a soft voice again.
