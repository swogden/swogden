Genesis

Chapter Four

King James Version
And Adam knew Eve his wife; and she conceived, and bare Cain, and said, I have gotten a man from the LORD. 2And she again bare his brother Abel. And Abel was a keeper of sheep, but Cain was a tiller of the ground.

3And in process of time it came to pass, that Cain brought of the fruit of the ground an offering unto the LORD. 4And Abel, he also brought of the firstlings of his flock and of the fat thereof. And the LORD had respect unto Abel and to his offering: 5But unto Cain and to his offering he had not respect. And Cain was very wroth, and his countenance fell.

6And the LORD said unto Cain, Why art thou wroth? and why is thy countenance fallen? 7If thou doest well, shalt thou not be accepted? and if thou doest not well, sin lieth at the door. And unto thee shall be his desire, and thou shalt rule over him.

8And Cain talked with Abel his brother: and it came to pass, when they were in the field, that Cain rose up against Abel his brother, and slew him.

9And the LORD said unto Cain, Where is Abel thy brother? And he said, I know not: Am I my brother's keeper? 10And he said, What hast thou done? the voice of thy brother's blood crieth unto me from the ground. 11And now art thou cursed from the earth, which hath opened her mouth to receive thy brother's blood from thy hand; 12When thou tillest the ground, it shall not henceforth yield unto thee her strength; a fugitive and a vagabond shalt thou be in the earth.

13And Cain said unto the LORD, My punishment is greater than I can bear. 14Behold, thou hast driven me out this day from the face of the earth; and from thy face shall I be hid; and I shall be a fugitive and a vagabond in the earth; and it shall come to pass, that every one that findeth me shall slay me. 15And the LORD said unto him, Therefore whosoever slayeth Cain, vengeance shall be taken on him sevenfold. And the LORD set a mark upon Cain, lest any finding him should kill him.

16And Cain went out from the presence of the LORD, and dwelt in the land of Nod, on the east of Eden.

17And Cain knew his wife; and she conceived, and bare Enoch: and he builded a city, and called the name of the city, after the name of his son, Enoch. 18And unto Enoch was born Irad: and Irad begat Mehujael: and Mehujael begat Methusael: and Methusael begat Lamech.

19And Lamech took unto him two wives: the name of the one was Adah, and the name of the other Zillah. 20And Adah bare Jabal: he was the father of such as dwell in tents, and of such as have cattle. 21And his brother's name was Jubal: he was the father of all such as handle the harp and organ. 22And Zillah, she also bare Tubalcain, an instructer of every artificer in brass and iron: and the sister of Tubalcain was Naamah.

23And Lamech said unto his wives, Adah and Zillah, Hear my voice; ye wives of Lamech, hearken unto my speech: for I have slain a man to my wounding, and a young man to my hurt. 24If Cain shall be avenged sevenfold, truly Lamech seventy and sevenfold.

25And Adam knew his wife again; and she bare a son, and called his name Seth: For God, said she, hath appointed me another seed instead of Abel, whom Cain slew. 26And to Seth, to him also there was born a son; and he called his name Enos: then began men to call upon the name of the LORD.

Commentary: Two Altars, a Voice from the Ground, and a Seed Appointed

Two Altars, Two Hearts Not Adam's Son: The Genealogy Test Out From the Presence of the Lord The Blood That Speaks The Mark and the Line of Lamech Seth, Appointed Instead
This chapter is where the enmity of Genesis 3:15 stops being a promise and starts being a family. Two brothers, one mother, two altars, and only one accepted — and Brother Branham read nearly every detail here, from the offerings to the genealogy at the end, as further confirmation of what was already true from the moment Cain was conceived: that this was never simply two brothers with different temperaments, but two seeds.

Two Altars, Two Hearts

Verses 3 and 4 put two offerings side by side without editorial comment — fruit from Cain, firstlings from Abel — and let the LORD's response supply the verdict. Brother Branham pictured the scene in detail, and pressed hard on why a sincere, hard-working, religious man's offering could still be refused.
“
“Here comes Cain. He probably worked all year, toiling, doing everything he could to get the very biggest apples, or the very biggest pumpkins, or whatever he had, brought it up to the gate. Built him an altar right there by the side of the gate, in the Presence of God. Put all of his fruits, and the big calla lilies and everything, and laid them on the altar correctly, then knelt down and worshipped God.”
— William Branham, “The Cruelty of Sin and the Penalty That It Cost” (53-0403, para. 109)
By every outward measure — an altar, a sacrifice, a posture of worship — Cain did what a worshiper does. That, Brother Branham insisted, is exactly the point: sincerity and effort were never what verse 4 says God was looking for.
“
“If God only requires you to go to church, Cain was just as just as Abel was. Cain built an altar unto the Lord…He brought a sacrifice. He worshipped the Lord. He knelt down and offered praise unto the Lord, and said, ‘Lord, here am I. Here am I, and I've brought You an offering. I've built an altar.’ Amen…And God turned His back on it.”
— William Branham, “The Cruelty of Sin and the Penalty That It Cost” (53-0403, paras. 111–113)
What Cain brought was the fruit of his own labor — the ground he had tilled, the best he personally could produce. What Abel brought cost him a life that was not his own to begin with. Brother Branham read that difference as the whole issue, plainly stated.
“
“Those charitable things are fine, but…that's not God's provided way yet. Cain come his own way. And many, tonight, are coming their own way.”
— William Branham, “The Cruelty of Sin and the Penalty That It Cost” (53-0403, para. 120)
“
“Now, Cain said, ‘I've laid all this down.’ God refused it. He made it all pretty.”
— William Branham, “The Cruelty of Sin and the Penalty That It Cost” (53-0403, para. 130)
Type & Shadow — Two Altars, One Way
Cain's altar is the fig leaf of Genesis 3 grown into a religion: sincere, costly to him personally, and still refused, because it was his own effort rather than a substitute death. Abel's lamb is the coat of skins carried forward one generation — blood standing in for the worshiper. The pattern set in the garden repeats at the very next altar humanity ever builds.

Not Adam's Son: The Genealogy Test

Brother Branham pointed to something structural in Scripture itself as confirmation of Cain's paternity: the genealogies that trace Adam's line down through Seth — the line Luke's Gospel eventually runs all the way to Christ — do not include Cain at any point.
“
“Now, she doesn't have a seed. Therefore, how she got this seed was Satan's seed. Cain was Satan's son. She had no seed, so she received it through sexual intercourse. And when she did it, it was given to her by God's enemy.”
— William Branham, “Questions and Answers 3” (64-0830M, para. 321)
“
“Luke comes over and names it right back again, and puts down from Adam. Not one time is Cain ever mentioned in the whole thing…the Bible said that, in the genealogies, that Adam begot his son, Seth…and not one time was Cain ever mentioned. Then he could not be the son of Adam.”
— William Branham, “Questions and Answers 3” (64-0830M, para. 326)
“
“Where did Cain come in? Where's Cain at, the first born? The birthright's to him…I want to see somebody put him in there now and say it wasn't the serpent's seed. It wasn't recognized in the genealogies of God's, or neither the genealogies of the human race…He was not; he was the serpent's seed and not Adam's seed!”
— William Branham, “Questions and Answers 3” (64-0830M, paras. 329–330)
Cain was the firstborn — the one who, by ordinary right, would open any list of Adam's descendants. His complete absence from that list, Brother Branham argued, is not an oversight but a record of whose son he actually was.

Out From the Presence of the Lord

Verse 16 — “Cain went out from the presence of the LORD” — opens a section of the chapter Brother Branham treated as far more than a change of address. He read the whole genealogy that follows, culminating in the builders, herders, musicians, and metalworkers of verses 17 through 22, as the record of what a line walking outside God's presence produces.
“
“‘Cain went out from the Presence of the Lord.’ There he made his mistake. And there is where you will make a mistake, and there is where I'll make the mistake, the very minute we walk out of the Presence of God.”
— William Branham, “Power of Transformation” (65-1031M, para. 145)
“
“As soon as they went out from the Presence of the Lord, they started building cities, they started making instruments; they started in science, to making brass and iron, and—and playing music, and so forth…Now where did it come from? Who went out? Cain, the serpent's seed.”
— William Branham, “Power of Transformation” (65-1031M, para. 147)
On this reading, verses 17 through 22 are not incidental genealogy but a doctrine in miniature: the line that walked out from God's presence became the line of cities, invention, music, and metalwork — remarkable in every worldly sense, and rooted in a walking-away rather than a walking-with.

The Blood That Speaks

Verses 9 and 10 record the shortest and most exposed exchange in the chapter — a direct question, an evasive lie, and then God naming what He already knows. Brother Branham took the LORD's answer in verse 10 as more than a figure of speech.
“
“Genesis 4:10…God said that—that…‘Abel's blood spoke out from the earth.’ We find out in Hebrews 12, that, ‘The Blood of the covenant of Christ speaks better things than that of Abel.’ We find out, the Blood does speak, It speaks in your behalf.”
— William Branham, “The Token” (64-0308, para. 134)
“
“If you want to put that down, Genesis 4:10. God said, ‘What about your brother?’ Said, ‘His blood cries from the earth, against you.’ That right? His blood is speaking…Abel, he was a righteous man. He died, innocent, because he was in the Way…The justice blood of Abel cried out against Cain. But the Blood of Jesus Christ, not only cried out, It redeemed…The blood of Abel could not hide Cain, see, but the Blood of Jesus can.”
— William Branham, “Token” (63-0901M, paras. 355, 358–359)
Type & Shadow — The Voice of the Blood
Abel's blood cries out from the ground for justice, and can only accuse. Hebrews 12:24 sets Christ's blood directly against it as a contrast, not a repetition: His speaks a better word — not vengeance, but mercy. The first blood ever shed by violence, in the very next generation after Eden, already points forward to the one Blood that would answer it.

The Mark and the Line of Lamech

God's mercy toward Cain in verse 15 — a mark meant to protect a murderer's life, not to punish him further — sits uneasily beside what his line produces a few generations later. By Lamech, the seventh from Adam counting through Cain, the pattern has hardened into open boasting over bloodshed and a marriage arrangement outside the one man, one wife pattern of Genesis 2. Whatever mercy verse 15 extended to Cain personally, verses 19 and 23 through 24 show it was not inherited as humility.

Seth, Appointed Instead

Verse 25 answers the chapter's violence with a replacement, in Eve's own words: God has appointed her another seed instead of Abel. Brother Branham underlined both halves of that sentence — that Seth stands in Abel's place, and that he is called a seed in exactly the sense verse 15 of the previous chapter had promised.
“
“And Adam knew his wife again; and she bare a son, and called his name Seth: For God, said she, has appointed me another seed instead of Abel, whom Cain slew…See, Eve didn't have a seed. You know that. The woman doesn't have a seed, the female. She has an egg, but not a seed. But she…appointed him, a seed, see, appointed by God's appointment, she took the seed.”
— William Branham, “Power of Transformation” (65-1031M, paras. 154, 156)
He drew the same conclusion from the other side of the story, framing Cain and Seth's birth against each other almost as two answers to the same event.
“
“Remember, there is nowhere in the Bible that said Cain was Adam's son. Said, ‘He was of that evil one,’ not Adam, ‘the devil’…Not out of Cain's, that generation genealogy, but out of Seth's genealogy. So, ‘Cain is the evil one.’ There come the serpent.”
— William Branham, “The Invisible Union of the Bride of Christ” (65-1125, paras. 149–150)
The chapter closes on verse 26 with the first recorded calling upon the name of the LORD, in the days of Seth's own son Enos — the righteous line, cut off from Cain's line entirely, already beginning to do the one thing Cain's line of cities and instruments never does anywhere in this chapter: call on God by name.
