Genesis

Chapter Twelve

King James Version
Now the LORD had said unto Abram, Get thee out of thy country, and from thy kindred, and from thy father's house, unto a land that I will shew thee: 2And I will make of thee a great nation, and I will bless thee, and make thy name great; and thou shalt be a blessing: 3And I will bless them that bless thee, and curse him that curseth thee: and in thee shall all families of the earth be blessed.

4So Abram departed, as the LORD had spoken unto him; and Lot went with him: and Abram was seventy and five years old when he departed out of Haran. 5And Abram took Sarai his wife, and Lot his brother's son, and all their substance that they had gathered, and the souls that they had gotten in Haran; and they went forth to go into the land of Canaan; and into the land of Canaan they came. 6And Abram passed through the land unto the place of Sichem, unto the plain of Moreh. And the Canaanite was then in the land.

7And the LORD appeared unto Abram, and said, Unto thy seed will I give this land: and there builded he an altar unto the LORD, who appeared unto him. 8And he removed from thence unto a mountain on the east of Bethel, and pitched his tent, having Bethel on the west, and Hai on the east: and there he builded an altar unto the LORD, and called upon the name of the LORD. 9And Abram journeyed, going on still toward the south.

10And there was a famine in the land: and Abram went down into Egypt to sojourn there; for the famine was grievous in the land. 11And it came to pass, when he was come near to enter into Egypt, that he said unto Sarai his wife, Behold now, I know that thou art a fair woman to look upon: 12Therefore it shall come to pass, when the Egyptians shall see thee, that they shall say, This is his wife: and they will kill me, but they will save thee alive. 13Say, I pray thee, thou art my sister: that it may be well with me for thy sake; and my soul shall live because of thee.

14And it came to pass, that, when Abram was come into Egypt, the Egyptians beheld the woman that she was very fair. 15The princes also of Pharaoh saw her, and commended her before Pharaoh: and the woman was taken into Pharaoh's house. 16And he entreated Abram well for her sake: and he had sheep, and oxen, and he asses, and menservants, and maidservants, and she asses, and camels.

17And the LORD plagued Pharaoh and his house with great plagues because of Sarai Abram's wife. 18And Pharaoh called Abram, and said, What is this that thou hast done unto me? why didst thou not tell me that she was thy wife? 19Why saidst thou, She is my sister? so I might have taken her to me to wife: now therefore behold thy wife, take her, and go thy way. 20And Pharaoh commanded his men concerning him: and they sent him away, and with all that he had.

Commentary: Called Out, and Nearly Undone by a Famine

Called by Election Separation Before Blessing Into the Land, Building Altars A Famine, and a Lapse of Faith
Genesis 12 opens the story Genesis 11 set up: out of Ur's idolatry and Babel's ruin, God calls one man and makes him the hinge of the rest of Scripture. By the chapter's end, that same man has already faltered under pressure — a pattern this commentary, and Brother Branham, treats honestly rather than smoothing over.

Called by Election

Verses 1 through 3 record the call and the promise in the same breath: leave your country, and I will make you a great nation. Brother Branham read this call as unconditional and entirely of God's own initiative, not a reward for anything Abram had done.
“
“God called Abraham, not because he was any better than anyone else, but God called Abraham because He had elected Abraham. And He called him and gave him His covenant unconditionally.”
— William Branham, “The Calling of Abraham” (55-1116, para. 19)
He was careful to place this election against the backdrop just established in chapters 10 and 11: Abram was not called out of a righteous household, but out of the very idolatry Babel and Ur represented.
“
“God, by sovereign election called Abraham, not because he'd done anything. He had come down from the tower of Babel with his father, and his mother, his wife Sarah…he lived down in Shinar in the city of Ur, of the land of Chaldean, and God by election called Abraham.”
— William Branham, “The Calling of Abraham” (55-1116, para. 19)

Separation Before Blessing

The call itself is built entirely around separation — from country, from kindred, from his father's house. Brother Branham treated this as the pattern for every call of God, not a peculiarity of Abram's story.
“
“Abraham, as soon as God called him, He called him to separate himself…'Abraham, separate yourself from your kindred. I want you to sojourn in a strange land. You've never walked this way before, but I want you to separate yourself so you can walk with Me.'”
— William Branham, “The Calling of Abraham” (55-1116, para. 21)

Into the Land, Building Altars

Verses 4 through 9 record the journey itself: Abram at seventy-five, Lot coming along, the household's arrival at Shechem and then Bethel, and an altar built at each stop. The text notes almost in passing that "the Canaanite was then in the land" — a reminder that the descendants of the very line cursed in Genesis 9 already occupied the ground God was promising to Abram's seed. No sourced Branham commentary specific to the altars at Shechem and Bethel turned up in the corpus searched for this study; the pattern itself, though, is plain in the text: everywhere Abram pitched his tent as a stranger, he also built an altar, worshipping in a land that was not yet his by anything but promise.

A Famine, and a Lapse of Faith

Verses 10 through 20 turn from worship to crisis. A famine drives Abram down into Egypt, and there, afraid for his own life, he asks Sarai to say she is his sister — true as far as it went, since she was his half-sister, but a half-truth built to protect himself at her expense. Brother Branham did not excuse this as a minor detail; he named it plainly as Abram running from the very land God had just shown him, and treated it as the test that came right after the call to separation and belief.
“
“A famine come and Abraham run. Then after God appeared to him, coming back…He come into the promised land again. There he was a sojourner, built an altar to the Lord and worshipped the Lord.”
— William Branham, “Jehovah-Jireh” (56-0613, para. 22)
Nothing in the corpus searched for this study comments on the specific details of Pharaoh's household, the plagues sent because of Sarai, or Pharaoh's rebuke of Abram in verses 17 through 19, so this commentary states what the text itself records: God protected Sarai and exposed Abram's deception without waiting for Abram to fix it himself, and Pharaoh — a pagan king — is the one left correcting the man who carries God's promise. The chapter closes with Abram sent out of Egypt no worse for it materially, but having demonstrated, right after his highest moment of obedience, how far his faith still had to grow.
Type & Shadow — Altars in Canaan, Compromise in Egypt
The chapter's geography preaches its own sermon. In the land of promise, Abram builds altars and calls on the name of the LORD. The moment he leaves that land for Egypt under pressure, no altar is mentioned at all — only fear, half-truth, and a foreign king left to sort out the damage. Scripture consistently uses Egypt as a picture of the world and its resources, and this chapter's pattern — worship in the place of promise, compromise in the place of convenience — recurs throughout Abram's life and the rest of the Bible.

Scripture Cross-References

Genesis 9:25–27 — the curse on Canaan, whose descendants already held this land  •  Genesis 20:1–18 — the same deception repeated with Abimelech at Gerar  •  Hebrews 11:8–10 — by faith Abraham obeyed, not knowing whither he went  •  Galatians 3:8 — in thee shall all nations be blessed, the gospel preached beforehand to Abraham  •  Acts 7:2–4 — Stephen's retelling of the call out of Mesopotamia  •  Romans 4:16–17 — Abraham, the father of all who believe, called by grace
