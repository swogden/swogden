Genesis

Chapter Twenty-Eight

King James Version
And Isaac called Jacob, and blessed him, and charged him, and said unto him, Thou shalt not take a wife of the daughters of Canaan. 2Arise, go to Padanaram, to the house of Bethuel thy mother's father; and take thee a wife from thence of the daughters of Laban thy mother's brother. 3And God Almighty bless thee, and make thee fruitful, and multiply thee, that thou mayest be a multitude of people; 4And give thee the blessing of Abraham, to thee, and to thy seed with thee; that thou mayest inherit the land wherein thou art a stranger, which God gave unto Abraham. 5And Isaac sent away Jacob: and he went to Padanaram unto Laban, son of Bethuel the Syrian, the brother of Rebekah, Jacob's and Esau's mother.

6When Esau saw that Isaac had blessed Jacob, and sent him away to Padanaram, to take him a wife from thence; and that as he blessed him he gave him a charge, saying, Thou shalt not take a wife of the daughters of Canaan; 7And that Jacob obeyed his father and his mother, and was gone to Padanaram; 8And Esau seeing that the daughters of Canaan pleased not Isaac his father; 9Then went Esau unto Ishmael, and took unto the wives which he had Mahalath the daughter of Ishmael Abraham's son, the sister of Nebajoth, to be his wife.

10And Jacob went out from Beersheba, and went toward Haran. 11And he lighted upon a certain place, and tarried there all night, because the sun was set; and he took of the stones of that place, and put them for his pillows, and lay down in that place to sleep. 12And he dreamed, and behold a ladder set up on the earth, and the top of it reached to heaven: and behold the angels of God ascending and descending on it. 13And, behold, the LORD stood above it, and said, I am the LORD God of Abraham thy father, and the God of Isaac: the land whereon thou liest, to thee will I give it, and to thy seed; 14And thy seed shall be as the dust of the earth, and thou shalt spread abroad to the west, and to the east, and to the north, and to the south: and in thee and in thy seed shall all the families of the earth be blessed. 15And, behold, I am with thee, and will keep thee in all places whither thou goest, and will bring thee again into this land; for I will not leave thee, until I have done that which I have spoken to thee of.

16And Jacob awaked out of his sleep, and he said, Surely the LORD is in this place; and I knew it not. 17And he was afraid, and said, How dreadful is this place! this is none other but the house of God, and this is the gate of heaven.

18And Jacob rose up early in the morning, and took the stone that he had put for his pillows, and set it up for a pillar, and poured oil upon the top of it. 19And he called the name of that place Bethel: but the name of that city was called Luz at the first.

20And Jacob vowed a vow, saying, If God will be with me, and will keep me in this way that I go, and will give me bread to eat, and raiment to put on, 21So that I come again to my father's house in peace; then shall the LORD be my God: 22And this stone, which I have set for a pillar, shall be God's house: and of all that God shall give me I will surely give the tenth.

Study: A Runaway, Anchored Anyway

This time there's no disguise, no goat skins, no deception. Isaac calls Jacob and blesses him a second time, openly and deliberately, sending him off to find a wife among his mother's own people rather than the Canaanites — the same instruction Abraham gave his own servant back in chapter 24. Whatever happened in chapter 27, Jacob leaves this scene with his father's genuine blessing spoken over him in full view.
Esau's reaction to watching all this is quietly telling. Realizing his Canaanite wives displeased his parents, he goes and marries into Ishmael's family instead — technically staying "in the family," but reaching for the branch of it that was never the line of promise to begin with. It reads like a man trying to fix a problem without actually understanding what the problem was.
Jacob, meanwhile, is alone on the road, fleeing a brother who's promised to kill him the moment their father dies. He stops for the night in open country with nothing but a stone for a pillow — about as far from a place of honor or safety as this story has put anyone yet.
“
“Take, for instance, the patriarch Jacob. He was a wondering boy; he was run away boy; he'd run away from home because he was afraid that Esau was going to kill him because of something that he had done. And he run away from home. Yet that boy was anchored in God by a Divine promise... God calls sovereignly without anything you done about it; there's nothing you done.”
— William Branham, “The Infallibility of God's Spoken Word” (56-0404)
It's in exactly that condition — asleep, alone, unaware, fleeing the consequences of his own scheming — that Jacob receives one of the most striking visions in the whole Bible: a ladder reaching from earth into heaven, angels moving both up and down it, and God Himself standing above it, repeating the covenant given to Abraham and Isaac word for word, unprompted and unconditional.
One reading pushes past the image itself to ask where that ladder is actually anchored — and argues it was never really secured in Jacob at all.
“
“He couldn't hook it on Jacob, the supplanter... He couldn't hook it on the temple... He tried it on the patriarch David... Everything that He tried to hook it to, it wouldn't anchor. But the prophet said, ‘There'll be a stone laid in Zion, a precious stone, a tried stone.’ That'll be the anchor... God, being God in heaven, with the ladder hooked into His own Throne, He hooked it also in none other but the Son of man, Jesus Christ.”
— William Branham, “The Infallibility of God's Spoken Word” (56-0404)
That reading connects Jacob's dream directly to something Jesus says centuries later in John's Gospel — that Nathanael, and everyone after him, would see "the angels of God ascending and descending upon the Son of man." Whatever a reader makes of that connection, the ladder itself in Genesis 28 isn't something Jacob builds, earns, or even asks for. He simply wakes up in the middle of it.
His own response afterward is disarmingly honest: "Surely the LORD is in this place; and I knew it not." He wasn't looking for God out here. He was running from his brother. And yet the vow he makes in response is strikingly conditional compared to the promise he was just given — "if God will be with me... then shall the LORD be my God" — a runaway still negotiating terms with the very God who just told him, unconditionally, that He already would be.
