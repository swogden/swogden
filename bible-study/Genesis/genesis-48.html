Genesis

Chapter Forty-Eight

King James Version
And it came to pass after these things, that one told Joseph, Behold, thy father is sick: and he took with him his two sons, Manasseh and Ephraim. 2And one told Jacob, and said, Behold, thy son Joseph cometh unto thee: and Israel strengthened himself, and sat upon the bed.

3And Jacob said unto Joseph, God Almighty appeared unto me at Luz in the land of Canaan, and blessed me, 4And said unto me, Behold, I will make thee fruitful, and multiply thee, and I will make of thee a multitude of people; and will give this land to thy seed after thee for an everlasting possession. 5And now thy two sons, Ephraim and Manasseh, which were born unto thee in the land of Egypt before I came unto thee into Egypt, are mine; as Reuben and Simeon, they shall be mine. 6And thy issue, which thou begettest after them, shall be thine, and shall be called after the name of their brethren in their inheritance. 7And as for me, when I came from Padan, Rachel died by me in the land of Canaan in the way, when yet there was but a little way to come unto Ephrath: and I buried her there in the way of Ephrath; the same is Bethlehem.

8And Israel beheld Joseph's sons, and said, Who are these? 9And Joseph said unto his father, They are my sons, whom God hath given me in this place. And he said, Bring them, I pray thee, unto me, and I will bless them. 10Now the eyes of Israel were dim for age, so that he could not see. And he brought them near unto him; and he kissed them, and embraced them. 11And Israel said unto Joseph, I had not thought to see thy face: and, lo, God hath shewed me also thy seed. 12And Joseph brought them out from between his knees, and he bowed himself with his face to the earth.

13And Joseph took them both, Ephraim in his right hand toward Israel's left hand, and Manasseh in his left hand toward Israel's right hand, and brought them near unto him. 14And Israel stretched out his right hand, and laid it upon Ephraim's head, who was the younger, and his left hand upon Manasseh's head, guiding his hands wittingly; for Manasseh was the firstborn.

15And he blessed Joseph, and said, God, before whom my fathers Abraham and Isaac did walk, the God which fed me all my life long unto this day, 16The Angel which redeemed me from all evil, bless the lads; and let my name be named on them, and the name of my fathers Abraham and Isaac; and let them grow into a multitude in the midst of the earth.

17And when Joseph saw that his father laid his right hand upon the head of Ephraim, it displeased him: and he held up his father's hand, to remove it from Ephraim's head unto Manasseh's head. 18And Joseph said unto his father, Not so, my father: for this is the firstborn; put thy right hand upon his head. 19And his father refused, and said, I know it, my son, I know it: he also shall become a people, and he also shall be great: but truly his younger brother shall be greater than he, and his seed shall become a multitude of nations. 20And he blessed them that day, saying, In thee shall Israel bless, saying, God make thee as Ephraim and as Manasseh: and he set Ephraim before Manasseh.

21And Israel said unto Joseph, Behold, I die: but God shall be with you, and bring you again unto the land of your fathers. 22Moreover I have given to thee one portion above thy brethren, which I took out of the hand of the Amorite with my sword and with my bow.

Commentary: The Crossed Hands

God Almighty Appeared unto Me at Luz Adopted as Reuben and Simeon The Crossed Hands A Transparency Note One Portion Above Thy Brethren

God Almighty Appeared unto Me at Luz

No sourced Branham commentary on verses 3–7 turned up in the corpus searched for this study. Jacob opens by recalling the vision at Luz — the same place, renamed Bethel, where he first fled his brother's anger with only a stone for a pillow (Genesis 28:10–19), and where he returned decades later and received the name Israel a second time (Genesis 35:9–15). His mention of Rachel's death "in the way to Ephrath" recalls that same return journey, when the one loss of his homecoming was the wife for whom he had labored fourteen years (Genesis 35:16–20). Jacob is, in effect, handing Joseph the whole of his own life's central promise and central grief in a single breath before he does anything else in this chapter.

Adopted as Reuben and Simeon

No sourced Branham commentary on verses 5–6 turned up in the corpus searched for this study. Jacob's declaration that Ephraim and Manasseh "shall be mine, as Reuben and Simeon" is a formal adoption: Joseph's two Egyptian-born sons are elevated to full tribal status alongside their uncles, while any further sons Joseph might have would be counted under Ephraim's or Manasseh's name rather than receiving tribes of their own. This is the reason twelve tribes of Israel can be counted in more than one way in later Scripture — sometimes with Levi included and Joseph counted once, and sometimes with Levi set apart and Ephraim and Manasseh counted as two separate tribes in his place (compare Numbers 1:32–35, where both appear individually).
Type & Shadow: The Crossed Hands
The one scene in this chapter that drew sustained attention in the corpus searched for this study is the blessing itself — Jacob's hands crossing so that the right-hand blessing, by custom due the firstborn, falls instead on the younger son. Brother Branham returned to this scene independently in at least two sermons, three years apart, reading it as a picture of the blessing and birthright passing from natural Israel, the elder, to the Gentile church, the younger — a transfer he ties to the cross itself, since Jacob's own hands were physically crossed in the act of blessing.
“
And when Joseph, rejected by his brethren, he was given a Gentile wife…and he bore Gentile children, half Gentile and Jew. They give a great symbol. When Jacob was blessing them, Ephraim on one side and Manasseh on the other, he crossed his hands and give the younger child the blessing…And Joseph, his prophet son, standing there, said, “Father, you've done wrong.” Said, “You put your right hand blessing on the young child, where it ought to have went on the old.” And he said, “I know my hands is crossed, but God has crossed them.” Why? Israel, having the rights to be a Bride, rejected and sold their birthrights, and the…went from the old son, Israel, to the new, Gentile. And the blessings went from there, through the cross, to the Gentile.
— William Branham, “The First Seal” (63-0318, paras. 239–240)
Three years earlier, the same scene had already appeared in a different sermon, with the same typology of blessing crossing over "through the Cross" from Jew to Gentile:
“
When Joseph then…after he married a Gentile and received a family, Ephraim and Manasseh, his sons…Jacob started to bless Ephraim and Manasseh. When he started to put his hands, he put Ephraim on the right, Manasseh on the left, to get the right hand blessing the oldest. But when he started to pray, his hands crossed, and he gave the youngest one the right-hand blessing instead of the one that was on the right hand. And Joseph said, “Not so, Father.” Said, “You have put the blessing on Manasseh instead of on Ephraim.” And he said, “God has crossed my hand.” What? From the Jews, the oldest, the first-chosen of God, through the Cross come the blessing back to the Gentiles, to get the Bride.
— William Branham, “The Revelation of Jesus Christ” (60-1204M, paras. 327–329)
A transparency note: these two accounts of Joseph's objection do not agree on which son's name belongs where, and one of them is internally inconsistent with itself. Genesis 48:17–18 states plainly that the right-hand blessing landed on Ephraim, the younger, and that Joseph asked his father to move it onto Manasseh, the firstborn. The 1963 sermon above states it exactly this way: the right hand went on "the young child," and Joseph said it "ought to have went on the old." The 1960 sermon's own narration (paragraph 327) also gets this right, describing Manasseh as the one positioned "on the right" who was passed over. But the quotation attributed to Joseph in the very next paragraph reverses the two names, having him say, "You have put the blessing on Manasseh instead of on Ephraim" — the opposite of both Genesis 48 and Brother Branham's own preceding sentence. Both quotations are preserved here exactly as spoken. The discrepancy affects only which name is attached to which role in that one recollected sentence; it does not touch the typological point being made in either sermon, which concerns the crossing of the hands itself and rests equally whichever way the names fall.

One Portion Above Thy Brethren

No sourced Branham commentary on verses 20–22 turned up in the corpus searched for this study. Jacob's closing words to Joseph include a claim the rest of Genesis does not otherwise record: "one portion above thy brethren, which I took out of the hand of the Amorite with my sword and with my bow." The only Amorite land Genesis has previously connected to Jacob's family at Shechem was purchased with money, not won by force (Genesis 33:18–19), and the one act of violence at Shechem was carried out by Simeon and Levi against Jacob's own wishes, drawing his rebuke rather than his participation (Genesis 34:25–30). Interpreters have long read verse 22 as Jacob speaking prophetically of a future conquest his descendants would carry out in his name, or as referring to an otherwise unrecorded skirmish; either way, the plot in question is generally identified with the field at Shechem, which does become Joseph's own burial place generations later (Joshua 24:32).
