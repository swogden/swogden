Chapter Fourteen

King James Version
And it came to pass in the days of Amraphel king of Shinar, Arioch king of Ellasar, Chedorlaomer king of Elam, and Tidal king of nations; 2That these made war with Bera king of Sodom, and with Birsha king of Gomorrah, Shinab king of Admah, and Shemeber king of Zeboiim, and the king of Bela, which is Zoar. 3All these were joined together in the vale of Siddim, which is the salt sea. 4Twelve years they served Chedorlaomer, and in the thirteenth year they rebelled. 5And in the fourteenth year came Chedorlaomer, and the kings that were with him, and smote the Rephaims in Ashteroth Karnaim, and the Zuzims in Ham, and the Emims in Shaveh Kiriathaim, 6And the Horites in their mount Seir, unto Elparan, which is by the wilderness. 7And they returned, and came to Enmishpat, which is Kadesh, and smote all the country of the Amalekites, and also the Amorites, that dwelt in Hazezontamar.

8And there went out the king of Sodom, and the king of Gomorrah, and the king of Admah, and the king of Zeboiim, and the king of Bela (the same is Zoar;) and they joined battle with them in the vale of Siddim; 9With Chedorlaomer the king of Elam, and with Tidal king of nations, and Amraphel king of Shinar, and Arioch king of Ellasar; four kings with five. 10And the vale of Siddim was full of slimepits; and the kings of Sodom and Gomorrah fled, and fell there; and they that remained fled to the mountain. 11And they took all the goods of Sodom and Gomorrah, and all their victuals, and went their way. 12And they took Lot, Abram's brother's son, who dwelt in Sodom, and his goods, and departed.

13And there came one that had escaped, and told Abram the Hebrew; for he dwelt in the plain of Mamre the Amorite, brother of Eshcol, and brother of Aner: and these were confederate with Abram. 14And when Abram heard that his brother was taken captive, he armed his trained servants, born in his own house, three hundred and eighteen, and pursued them unto Dan. 15And he divided himself against them, he and his servants, by night, and smote them, and pursued them unto Hobah, which is on the left hand of Damascus. 16And he brought back all the goods, and also brought again his brother Lot, and his goods, and the women also, and the people.

17And the king of Sodom went out to meet him after his return from the slaughter of Chedorlaomer, and of the kings that were with him, at the valley of Shaveh, which is the king's dale. 18And Melchizedek king of Salem brought forth bread and wine: and he was the priest of the most high God. 19And he blessed him, and said, Blessed be Abram of the most high God, possessor of heaven and earth: 20And blessed be the most high God, which hath delivered thine enemies into thy hand. And he gave him tithes of all.

21And the king of Sodom said unto Abram, Give me the persons, and take the goods to thyself. 22And Abram said to the king of Sodom, I have lift up mine hand unto the LORD, the most high God, the possessor of heaven and earth, 23That I will not take from a thread even to a shoelatchet, and that I will not take any thing that is thine, lest thou shouldest say, I have made Abram rich: 24Save only that which the young men have eaten, and the portion of the men which went with me, Aner, Eshcol, and Mamre; let them take their portion.

Study: The King With No Beginning

Genesis 14 opens with none of Abram's usual concerns — it's regional politics, four kings against five, years of tribute and rebellion, place names most readers will never encounter again. Abram doesn't even appear until verse 13, and only then because the war has swept up someone he cares about: Lot, still living in the very city whose wickedness chapter 13 had already flagged, is taken captive along with everything he owns. The choice Lot made by sight, two chapters ago, has just cost him his freedom.
Abram's response is immediate and decisive — no altar-building, no waiting on a word from God first, just three hundred and eighteen trained men and a night raid that recovers everyone and everything. This is a different Abram than the one who panicked in Egypt: quick, capable, and entirely undivided about defending his family.
Then, with no introduction and no explanation at all, the chapter's real turn arrives: "Melchizedek king of Salem brought forth bread and wine: and he was the priest of the most high God." No genealogy, no backstory — a figure this significant simply appears, blesses Abram, and (per Hebrews 7, written centuries later) vanishes from the text having raised more questions than the chapter answers. Brother Branham addressed the question of his identity directly and at length.
“
“There is several schools of thought on Him. One... claims He's just a myth... And the others says it was a priesthood... It can't be that, for in the 4th verse it says He was a Person, a ‘Man.’ So He was not just a priesthood order, neither was He a myth. He was a Person. And the Person is Eternal... He had no father. He had no mother. And He had no time He ever began. And He had no time He ever ended... So It would have to be an Eternal Person... that's God, 'cause He's the only One that's Eternal.”
— William Branham, “Who Is This Melchisedec?” (65-0221E)
Having ruled out "myth" and "priestly office" as explanations, that leaves Melchizedek, in this reading, as nothing less than a pre-incarnate appearance of Christ Himself — a conclusion Brother Branham draws even more directly elsewhere, tying it to what Melchizedek actually does in the text: not offering money for the rescued goods, but bread and wine.
“
“Melchisedec only offered him ‘bread and wine.’ ...The King of peace, the King of righteousness, met Abraham as he returned. And give him... the Communion, ‘bread and wine,’ thousands of years... before bread and wine was ever an ordinance in the Church... Who was this Priest? None other than the incarnated Lord Jesus Christ, in His magnificent power.”
— William Branham, “Melchisedec, the Great Prince and King” (55-0109M)
Whatever a reader concludes about Melchizedek's exact identity, the text is clear about Abram's response: he gives this stranger a tenth of everything, centuries before Israel has a priesthood, a temple, or a law requiring tithes at all. It's given freely, to someone who asked for nothing and offered a blessing instead.
That makes what happens next, only two verses later, land as a deliberate contrast rather than a coincidence. The king of Sodom — whose own city Abram has just risked his life to rescue — offers him all the recovered goods outright. Abram refuses everything, down to a literal shoelace.
“
“The king of Sodom come out... and said, ‘All of the goods that you have taken, that these people took away, I'll give them to you.’ ...Abraham said, ‘I won't take no thread, to a shoelatchet; that you might say you made Abraham rich.’”
— William Branham, “Melchisedec, the Great Prince and King” (55-0109M)
Two kings meet Abram within a handful of verses of each other. One offers wealth with an unspoken price attached — the right to say afterward that he made Abram rich. The other offers bread, wine, and a blessing, and asks nothing at all except worship. Abram accepts everything from the second and refuses to take even a shoelace from the first, and the chapter lets that contrast speak for itself without further comment.
