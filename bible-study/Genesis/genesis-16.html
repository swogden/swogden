Genesis

Chapter Sixteen

King James Version
Now Sarai Abram's wife bare him no children: and she had an handmaid, an Egyptian, whose name was Hagar. 2And Sarai said unto Abram, Behold now, the LORD hath restrained me from bearing: I pray thee, go in unto my maid; it may be that I may obtain children by her. And Abram hearkened to the voice of Sarai. 3And Sarai Abram's wife took Hagar her maid the Egyptian, after Abram had dwelt ten years in the land of Canaan, and gave her to her husband Abram to be his wife. 4And he went in unto Hagar, and she conceived: and when she saw that she had conceived, her mistress was despised in her eyes.

5And Sarai said unto Abram, My wrong be upon thee: I have given my maid into thy bosom; and when she saw that she had conceived, I was despised in her eyes: the LORD judge between me and thee. 6But Abram said unto Sarai, Behold, thy maid is in thy hand; do to her as it pleaseth thee. And when Sarai dealt hardly with her, she fled from her face.

7And the angel of the LORD found her by a fountain of water in the wilderness, by the fountain in the way to Shur. 8And he said, Hagar, Sarai's maid, whence camest thou? and whither wilt thou go? And she said, I flee from the face of my mistress Sarai. 9And the angel of the LORD said unto her, Return to thy mistress, and submit thyself under her hands. 10And the angel of the LORD said unto her, I will multiply thy seed exceedingly, that it shall not be numbered for multitude.

11And the angel of the LORD said unto her, Behold, thou art with child, and shalt bear a son, and shalt call his name Ishmael; because the LORD hath heard thy affliction. 12And he will be a wild man; his hand will be against every man, and every man's hand against him; and he shall dwell in the presence of all his brethren.

13And she called the name of the LORD that spake unto her, Thou God seest me: for she said, Have I also here looked after him that seeth me? 14Wherefore the well was called Beerlahairoi; behold, it is between Kadesh and Bered.

15And Hagar bare Abram a son: and Abram called his son's name, which Hagar bare, Ishmael. 16And Abram was fourscore and six years old, when Hagar bare Ishmael to Abram.

Commentary: A Promise Rushed, and a Seed That Wasn't the Seed

Sarai's Plan The Angel at the Well A Wild Man, Not the Promise The Bondwoman and the Freewoman
Ten years into the promise of chapter 15, still no child has come, and Genesis 16 records what happens when two people try to hurry God's timing along by entirely natural means. What looks at first like a reasonable solution to a childless marriage becomes, in Brother Branham's reading, the pattern behind one of the New Testament's most important allegories.

Sarai's Plan

Verses 1 through 6 lay the plan out plainly: Sarai proposes that Abram father a child through her handmaid Hagar, and Abram agrees. Brother Branham read Sarai's proposal as doubt dressed up as practicality — not unbelief that God would keep His word, but a fear that He could not do it the way He had said.
“
“One, by Hagar, which was his wife's maid…And Sarah, thinking that God would not be able to keep all of His promise true, she told Abraham…”
— William Branham, “The Seed Is Not Heir With the Shuck” (65-0218, para. 46)
He returned to this same moment elsewhere, framing it not as Abram's failure of faith but specifically as Sarah's.
“
“Sarah doubted God's Word, that God was able to keep It…Abraham did not doubt it. Sarah did doubt it; she was the one. It was not Adam that doubted; it was Eve that doubted.”
— William Branham, “The Seed Is Not Heir With the Shuck” (65-0218, para. 60)

The Angel at the Well

Verses 7 through 14 follow Hagar into the wilderness after Sarai's harsh treatment drives her away, and record the first appearance in Scripture of the phrase "the angel of the LORD" finding a person by name and by name of relation — "Hagar, Sarai's maid." He sends her back, but not without a promise of his own: a son, a name, and a multitude of descendants too many to number. Hagar's own response — naming God "Thou God seest me" — is one of the most personal moments in Genesis, spoken by a servant with no standing in the covenant at all. No sourced Branham commentary on the specific wording of the angel's promise or Hagar's naming of the well turned up in the corpus searched for this study, so this commentary states what the text itself records: God met this Egyptian servant in the wilderness by name, on her own account, before she had returned to anyone.

A Wild Man, Not the Promise

Verses 12, 15, and 16 describe the son who will be born, and the description is anything but a picture of the covenant heir chapter 15 promised: "a wild man," his hand against every man and every man's against him. Brother Branham read this as more than a personality sketch — a sign that Ishmael, however real and however loved by his father, was never the child the promise pointed to.
“
“Hagar, the second wife of Abraham…brought forth a man, but…the Bible said he was 'a wild man.' He lived by his bow, and no man would conquer him. He was untameable, unconvertible, unregenerated…because he was contrary to God's Word.”
— William Branham, “The Seed Is Not Heir With the Shuck” (65-0218, para. 72)
He drew the line just as sharply on the question of inheritance, tying it directly to Paul's own use of this chapter in Galatians.
“
“It could not be heir with Ishmael, the seed of the bondswoman…Neither can the natural be heir with the spiritual. No more can Ishmael's children be heir with Isaac's children, and no more than the carnal can be heir with the spiritual.”
— William Branham, “The Seed Is Not Heir With the Shuck” (65-0218, paras. 60–61)
Elsewhere he identified Ishmael's line historically, naming a people group he understood to descend through this son, while still holding a true, if incomplete, knowledge of God.
“
“That's Ishmael's children. See, Hagar, they're sons of Abraham out of Hagar…They believe in a true Jehovah God…And now, they was through Hagar, Ishmael, and come the Mohammedans.”
— William Branham, “Questions and Answers on Genesis” (53-0729, para. 231)
Type & Shadow — The Bondwoman and the Freewoman
Paul builds an entire allegory on this chapter in Galatians 4: Hagar as the covenant of law and bondage, Sarah as the covenant of promise and freedom, and the two sons as two ways of trying to inherit what God has promised — by human effort, or by faith alone. Brother Branham extended the picture further, describing Hagar, Sarah, and later Mary as three successive carriers of the promised Seed, each one closer to the true fulfillment than the last, but only the third — conceived by faith in the Word alone, with no human effort involved at all — actually bringing forth the promise itself.
Read this way, Genesis 16 is not simply a domestic dispute resolved with a servant's exile. It is the first time Scripture shows, in narrative form, what happens when God's people try to produce His promise by their own hand rather than waiting for it — a pattern chapter 21 will bring to its conclusion when Ishmael and Isaac can no longer live under the same roof.
