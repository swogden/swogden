Chapter Fourteen

King James Version
And it came to pass in the days of Amraphel king of Shinar, Arioch king of Ellasar, Chedorlaomer king of Elam, and Tidal king of nations; 2That these made war with Bera king of Sodom, and with Birsha king of Gomorrah, Shinab king of Admah, and Shemeber king of Zeboiim, and the king of Bela, which is Zoar. 3All these were joined together in the vale of Siddim, which is the salt sea. 4Twelve years they served Chedorlaomer, and in the thirteenth year they rebelled.

5And in the fourteenth year came Chedorlaomer, and the kings that were with him, and smote the Rephaims in Ashteroth Karnaim, and the Zuzims in Ham, and the Emims in Shaveh Kiriathaim, 6And the Horites in their mount Seir, unto Elparan, which is by the wilderness. 7And they returned, and came to Enmishpat, which is Kadesh, and smote all the country of the Amalekites, and also the Amorites, that dwelt in Hazezontamar.

8And there went out the king of Sodom, and the king of Gomorrah, and the king of Admah, and the king of Zeboiim, and the king of Bela (the same is Zoar;) and they joined battle with them in the vale of Siddim; 9With Chedorlaomer the king of Elam, and with Tidal king of nations, and Amraphel king of Shinar, and Arioch king of Ellasar; four kings with five. 10And the vale of Siddim was full of slimepits; and the kings of Sodom and Gomorrah fled, and fell there; and they that remained fled to the mountain. 11And they took all the goods of Sodom and Gomorrah, and all their victuals, and went their way. 12And they took Lot, Abram's brother's son, who dwelt in Sodom, and his goods, and departed.

13And there came one that had escaped, and told Abram the Hebrew; for he dwelt in the plain of Mamre the Amorite, brother of Eshcol, and brother of Aner: and these were confederate with Abram. 14And when Abram heard that his brother was taken captive, he armed his trained servants, born in his own house, three hundred and eighteen, and pursued them unto Dan. 15And he divided himself against them, he and his servants, by night, and smote them, and pursued them unto Hobah, which is on the left hand of Damascus. 16And he brought back all the goods, and also brought again his brother Lot, and his goods, and the women also, and the people.

17And the king of Sodom went out to meet him after his return from the slaughter of Chedorlaomer, and of the kings that were with him, at the valley of Shaveh, which is the king's dale. 18And Melchizedek king of Salem brought forth bread and wine: and he was the priest of the most high God. 19And he blessed him, and said, Blessed be Abram of the most high God, possessor of heaven and earth: 20And blessed be the most high God, which hath delivered thine enemies into thy hand. And he gave him tithes of all.

21And the king of Sodom said unto Abram, Give me the persons, and take the goods to thyself. 22And Abram said to the king of Sodom, I have lift up mine hand unto the LORD, the most high God, the possessor of heaven and earth, 23That I will not take from a thread even to a shoelatchet, and that I will not take any thing that is thine, lest thou shouldest say, I have made Abram rich: 24Save only that which the young men have eaten, and the portion of the men which went with me, Aner, Eshcol, and Mamre; let them take their portion.

Commentary: A Rescue, and a Priest Without a Genealogy

A War Among Petty Kings Three Hundred and Eighteen Melchizedek, Priest and King A Priest Without Beginning or End Abram's Refusal
Genesis 14 is unlike anything before it in the book — a battlefield report naming nine kings, followed by the sudden appearance of a figure the rest of Scripture will spend two more books explaining. Lot's choice in chapter 13 catches up with him here, and Abram's response introduces one of the most important types in the entire Bible.

A War Among Petty Kings

Verses 1 through 12 read like a chronicle: four eastern kings against five kings of the plain, a rebellion after twelve years of tribute, and a rout that ends with Sodom and Gomorrah's kings fleeing into slimepits while their cities are plundered. Lot is swept up in the disaster as a resident of Sodom, not as a combatant — the text is careful to call him "Abram's brother's son," tying him back to the family he had separated from in the previous chapter even as it records the consequence of where he chose to pitch his tent.

Three Hundred and Eighteen

Verses 13 through 16 turn from the kings' war to Abram's rescue. He hears that his brother is taken, arms his own trained servants — the text specifies three hundred and eighteen men, born in his own household — and pursues the four kings' armies all the way to Dan and beyond Damascus. Brother Branham marveled at what that number represented for a man who had come into the land with nothing.
“
“Just think of the man that had been a pauper when he left the land down there in Chaldea, of Ur, had three hundred and eighteen armed men as servants. Say God don't bless you? 'Three hundred and eighteen.'”
— William Branham, “Melchisedec, the Great Prince and King” (55-0109M, para. 143)
He read the whole rescue as a picture larger than the historical event itself — Abram going after his own backslidden household member, at real risk, rather than writing him off.
“
“Abraham; 'his brother' that had gone astray. A picture of the true Church, or the true believer of God, that's blessed of God, and has the promise of God…And he went after his backslidden brother, and pursued with a little handful of men…and overcome them, and took them and slew them. And brought back all that was lost.”
— William Branham, “Melchisedec, the Great Prince and King” (55-0109M, paras. 149, 151)

Melchizedek, Priest and King

Verses 17 through 20 introduce Melchizedek without any warning or background — he simply appears, king of Salem and "priest of the most high God," bringing out bread and wine to meet Abram returning from battle. He blesses Abram, blesses God for the victory, and receives a tenth of everything from him. Brother Branham treated this meeting as the single most important encounter in the chapter, identifying Melchizedek's gift as a picture centuries ahead of its time.
“
“Melchisedec did not offer him any money. Melchisedec only offered him 'bread and wine.' Melchisedec was a Priest of the Most High; the King of Jerusalem…And give him the Communion, bread and wine, thousands of years, or hundreds of years, before bread and wine was ever an ordinance in the Church.”
— William Branham, “Melchisedec, the Great Prince and King” (55-0109M, paras. 153, 155)

A Priest Without Beginning or End

What makes Melchizedek unlike every other priest in Scripture, Brother Branham pointed out, is what the text does not say about him: no father, no mother, no genealogy, no record of birth or death. Hebrews 7 draws directly on this silence to describe him as a picture of an eternal priesthood, and Brother Branham identified the figure behind that silence as Christ Himself, appearing to Abram before His incarnation.
“
“Without father, without mother…without beginning of days, or without ending of life…If He had no father, He had no mother, He had no beginning of days or ending of life, Melchisedec still exists…but was made like unto the Son of God; abideth a priest continually.”
— William Branham, “Melchisedec, the Great Prince and King” (55-0109M, paras. 159–160)
“
“Who was this Priest? None other than the incarnated Lord Jesus Christ, in His magnificent power. He was the same One…And He was in a shadow. That Melchisedec, when He become a Man of flesh, would be cut into part, and the Communion will be given, the fragments of His broken body, as we take today.”
— William Branham, “Melchisedec, the Great Prince and King” (55-0109M, para. 163)
Type & Shadow — Bread and Wine Before the Law
Centuries before Moses, before the Passover, before any Communion table existed in Israel's worship, a priest without recorded ancestry hands a returning warrior bread and wine. Brother Branham read this not as coincidence but as the same pattern appearing early — Christ's own broken body and shed blood, pictured in advance, offered by the same eternal Priest who would one day offer the reality it pointed to.

Abram's Refusal

Verses 21 through 24 close the chapter with a contrast the text sets up deliberately: the king of Sodom offers to let Abram keep all the recovered goods, and Abram refuses every bit of it, down to a thread or a shoelatchet, so that no one could ever say Sodom's king had made him rich. He asks nothing for himself, only the rightful share for the men who fought alongside him.
“
“Abraham said, 'I won't take no thread, to a shoelatchet; that you might say you made Abraham rich.'”
— William Branham, “Melchisedec, the Great Prince and King” (55-0109M, para. 147)
Read against the chapter's opening, the point stands out clearly: Abram had just received a blessing and bread and wine from the Priest of the Most High. Having already been blessed by God, he had nothing to gain and everything to lose by also being indebted to the king of Sodom.

Scripture Cross-References

Hebrews 7:1–3 — Melchizedek, without father, without mother, without descent  •  Hebrews 7:4–10 — the argument from Abraham's tithe to Melchizedek's greater priesthood  •  Psalm 110:4 — the LORD hath sworn, Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchizedek  •  Matthew 26:26–28 — the bread and the cup of the New Testament in Christ's blood  •  Genesis 13:12–13 — Lot's choice of Sodom, the seed of this chapter's danger  •  Genesis 18:1–8 — the three visitors at Mamre, read by Brother Branham as the same Person appearing again
