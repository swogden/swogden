Exploring the Sermons of William Branham · Study 02

Time-Tested Memorials Of God (57-0818)

Preached August 18, 1957 · Jeffersonville, Indiana

Time-Tested Memorials Of God asks what truly remains after time, pressure, and change have done their work. Brother Branham begins with Christ’s declaration in Matthew 24:35: heaven and earth will pass away, but God’s Word will not. From that foundation, the sermon traces memorials that either endure as witnesses to God’s faithfulness or stand as warnings of a life turned away from Him.

Sermon at a Glance

  • Title: Time-Tested Memorials Of God
  • Date: August 18, 1957
  • Location: Jeffersonville, Indiana
  • Opening text: Matthew 24:35
  • Central thought: God’s Word is the lasting memorial; a life tested and faithful becomes a witness to His grace.

The Measure of a Memorial

The sermon begins by recognizing that people naturally want to leave something behind: a marker, a shrine, a building, a name, or an accomplishment. Yet the passage of time exposes the difference between what merely looks permanent and what is truly enduring. Empires, temples, statues, and human achievements may be impressive, but they do not possess the permanence of God’s Word.

Christ’s words supply the measure: the creation itself will pass away, but His Word will not. Brother Branham therefore calls the Word the one memorial that has already endured every attempt to discredit, suppress, or replace it. A Christian life has lasting value only as it is built upon that same Word.

Joshua’s Stones at Jordan

Joshua 3 and 4 provide the sermon’s first great biblical memorial. Israel crossed the Jordan when the river was at flood stage. The priests went forward carrying the ark, and the waters stood back so the people could pass. Afterward, God commanded that stones be taken from the riverbed and set up as a memorial.

The stones had a purpose beyond the day of the crossing. Later generations would see them and ask why they were there. The answer would be a testimony: God had made a way where there was no natural way. In this study, the stacked stones are not simply an image of the past; they represent a remembered act of God that teaches those who come after.

Memorials That Warn

The sermon also considers memorials that warn rather than encourage. Lot’s wife is presented as a solemn example of someone who left Sodom physically but whose heart remained attached to what she had left behind. Her story warns against beginning a separation from the world while continuing to look back with longing.

This contrast is important. A memorial can point people toward faithfulness, or it can reveal the consequence of refusing God’s call. The question is not simply whether a person leaves a mark, but what that mark teaches the next generation about obedience, faith, and the condition of the heart.

What Time Cannot Keep

Brother Branham surveys famous monuments and rulers to make the point that human glory fades. Buildings crumble, statues are destroyed, and the ground eventually covers the places once identified with power. Even religious structures, however beautiful, cannot take the place of the living Word of God.

The sermon does not condemn ordinary provision for one’s family or the desire to do good work. Its emphasis is on proportion: temporary things should not displace eternal ones. The more enduring inheritance is a testimony that directs children and others to the saving grace and promises of God.

The Bridge and the Foundation

To explain a tested faith, the sermon turns to the building of a great bridge. The engineer would not build on shifting sand. The foundation had to reach solid rock, and each part of the structure had to be tested before it was trusted to carry weight. The bridge became a picture of confidence based on a sound foundation rather than wishful thinking.

Brother Branham applies this image to the Church. Christ is not building on personality, denominational loyalty, emotion, or human theology. The Church is founded on Christ and His Word. A believer is not made secure by avoiding every storm, but by being anchored where the storm cannot remove the foundation.

Trials Reveal the Material

The bridge illustration leads to the sermon’s central personal application: testing reveals what a person is made of. The language is not that every hardship is pleasant, but that trials can expose weak places and drive the believer to a deeper hold on God. The sermon compares this work to refining gold until impurities are removed and the refiner can see his reflection.

That reflection is the goal. The qualities named in the sermon are not display or reputation, but a life becoming peaceful, meek, gentle, humble, willing, and fixed on Christ. Testing is therefore not presented merely as punishment; it is a proving process for material that God can use.

The Church as Christ’s Memorial

The sermon’s highest point is that Christ is preparing a Church that will stand as a memorial of His grace. He gave Himself for a people He can present without spot or wrinkle. The Church is not an unfinished human project; it is the result of Christ’s sacrifice, His Word, and a people proven faithful through the tests of life.

A Testimony That Remains

Brother Branham returns to the practical question of what a person leaves behind. A home, savings, and possessions have their place, but they cannot replace the testimony of a life that trusted Christ through trial, sorrow, sickness, and death. The testimony that holds is the one that can still speak when the final hour comes: God’s grace has been sufficient.

That is the kind of memorial this sermon calls believers to become. Not a monument raised to self, but a life that points beyond itself—one whose faith, works, endurance, and love bear witness that Christ has kept His Word.

Study Reflection

Read Joshua 4 and identify the reason God gave for the memorial stones. Then consider what evidence in a believer’s life can tell others—not merely that hardship occurred, but that God’s Word held firm through it.
