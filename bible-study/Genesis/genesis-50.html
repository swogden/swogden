Chapter Fifty

King James Version
And Joseph fell upon his father's face, and wept upon him, and kissed him. 2And Joseph commanded his servants the physicians to embalm his father: and the physicians embalmed Israel. 3And forty days were fulfilled for him; for so are fulfilled the days of those which are embalmed: and the Egyptians mourned for him threescore and ten days.

4And when the days of his mourning were past, Joseph spake unto the house of Pharaoh, saying, If now I have found grace in your eyes, speak, I pray you, in the ears of Pharaoh, saying, 5My father made me swear, saying, Lo, I die: in my grave which I have digged for me in the land of Canaan, there shalt thou bury me. Now therefore let me go up, I pray thee, and bury my father, and I will come again. 6And Pharaoh said, Go up, and bury thy father, according as he made thee swear.

7And Joseph went up to bury his father: and with him went up all the servants of Pharaoh, the elders of his house, and all the elders of the land of Egypt, 8And all the house of Joseph, and his brethren, and his father's house: only their little ones, and their flocks, and their herds, they left in the land of Goshen. 9And there went up with him both chariots and horsemen: and it was a very great company.

10And they came to the threshingfloor of Atad, which is beyond Jordan, and there they mourned with a great and very sore lamentation: and he made a mourning for his father seven days. 11And when the inhabitants of the land, the Canaanites, saw the mourning in the floor of Atad, they said, This is a grievous mourning to the Egyptians: wherefore the name of it was called Abelmizraim, which is beyond Jordan.

12And his sons did unto him according as he commanded them: 13For his sons carried him into the land of Canaan, and buried him in the cave of the field of Machpelah, which Abraham bought with the field for a possession of a buryingplace of Ephron the Hittite, before Mamre. 14And Joseph returned into Egypt, he, and his brethren, and all that went up with him to bury his father, after he had buried his father.

15And when Joseph's brethren saw that their father was dead, they said, Joseph will peradventure hate us, and will certainly requite us all the evil which we did unto him. 16And they sent a messenger unto Joseph, saying, Thy father did command before he died, saying, 17So shall ye say unto Joseph, Forgive, I pray thee now, the trespass of thy brethren, and their sin; for they did unto thee evil: and now, we pray thee, forgive the trespass of the servants of the God of thy father. And Joseph wept when they spake unto him. 18And his brethren also went and fell down before his face; and they said, Behold, we be thy servants.

19And Joseph said unto them, Fear not: for am I in the place of God? 20But as for you, ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good, to bring to pass, as it is this day, to save much people alive. 21Now therefore fear ye not: I will nourish you, and your little ones. And he comforted them, and spake kindly unto them.

22And Joseph dwelt in Egypt, he, and his father's house: and Joseph lived an hundred and ten years. 23And Joseph saw Ephraim's children of the third generation: the children also of Machir the son of Manasseh were brought up upon Joseph's knees.

24And Joseph said unto his brethren, I die: and God will surely visit you, and bring you out of this land unto the land which he sware to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob. 25And Joseph took an oath of the children of Israel, saying, God will surely visit you, and ye shall carry up my bones from hence.

26So Joseph died, being an hundred and ten years old: and they embalmed him, and he was put in a coffin in Egypt.

Commentary: A Coffin in Egypt

The Mourning of Egypt Am I in the Place of God? The Bones That Waited

The Mourning of Egypt

No sourced Branham commentary on verses 1–14 turned up in the corpus searched for this study. This is the Bible's first detailed description of embalming, and the text reports it as an Egyptian medical procedure — "his servants the physicians" — rather than an Israelite custom; nothing elsewhere in Genesis suggests the patriarchs' bodies were treated this way in Canaan. The seventy days of mourning matched the length reserved in Egyptian custom for royalty, an honor Joseph's position secured for his father. The procession itself is notable for its size: Pharaoh sends "all the servants of Pharaoh, the elders of his house, and all the elders of the land of Egypt" along with Joseph's own household, leaving only the children and livestock behind in Goshen (verse 8) — a precaution that also, incidentally, guaranteed the family's return, since no one leaves an entire nation's flocks and herds behind for good. The mourning at the threshingfloor of Atad was conspicuous enough that the Canaanite residents renamed the site for it, "Abelmizraim," meaning "the mourning of Egypt" — the last place-name Genesis records being coined, and coined by outsiders watching a foreign nation grieve.

Am I in the Place of God?

No sourced Branham commentary on verses 15–21 turned up in the corpus searched for this study, despite searches run against its most familiar language, including "ye thought evil against me, but God meant it unto good." This is one of the most quoted verses in Genesis, and its absence from the corpus searched for this study is worth noting plainly rather than passing over. The brothers' fear here reveals that Joseph's earlier forgiveness in Genesis 45, however genuine, had not fully settled their own conscience; they still expect, seventeen years later and only after their father's protective presence is gone, that the reckoning might finally come. Joseph's answer refuses the role they are afraid he will take: "Am I in the place of God?" He will not withhold from them what only God has the right to withhold, and his theology of the whole preceding ordeal is stated here in its plainest form — not that the evil done to him was somehow not evil, but that God's purpose ran underneath it and beyond it the entire time.
Type & Shadow: The Bones That Waited
Joseph's closing instruction — that his own bones be carried out of Egypt whenever God visited his people — drew commentary in the corpus searched for this study, read as an act of resurrection faith rather than sentimental attachment to homeland. Brother Branham treated the patriarchs' insistence on burial in the land of promise, rather than in Egypt, as evidence that each of them died resting on a specific, spoken word from God they expected to see fulfilled even after death.
“
Then, Joseph, being a prophet. He prospered down in Egypt. He knew God. God had revealed Himself to him. And when he died, said, “Don't you bury my bones down here, but put…when…Someday God will surely visit you!” Why? He rested solemnly upon the Word of God to Moses, “Four hundred years they'll serve this nation, but I'll bring them out.” He rested solemnly upon the Word.
— William Branham, “The Entombment” (57-0420, para. 36)
Eight years later, preaching on the resurrection, he returned to the same scene at greater length, tracing it back through Jacob's earlier oath to Joseph (Genesis 47:29–31) before arriving at Joseph's own instruction concerning his bones:
“
Jacob died, way down in Egypt. But being a prophet, now, with this potential, this quickening power, he said, “Don't you bury me down here, Joseph…Swear to the God of Heaven you'll not bury me down here.”…Joseph, being a prophet, also, laid his hands upon his crippled daddy. Said, “I swear by the God of Abraham, Isaac, of Jacob, I'll not bury you here.” They took him up and buried him up in that land…When Joseph died, he said, “Don't you bury me down here. Don't bury me down here.” Why? God is God everywhere, but He has a plan. Joseph was a prophet. He said, “Someday,” listen, his words, “the Lord God will vis-it you, and will take you out of this land. And when you go, you take my bones.” That quickening power was in them bones.
— William Branham, “The Easter Seal” (65-0410, paras. 203–207)
Both sermons read Joseph's request in light of what actually happened centuries later: his bones were carried up out of Egypt at the Exodus (Exodus 13:19) and finally buried at Shechem (Joshua 24:32), a promise kept across a longer span of time than any other recorded in the patriarchal narratives.

The Close of Genesis

Genesis ends where it must: not in the land God promised Abraham, but in Egypt, with a coffin. Every major figure introduced across fifty chapters — Adam, Noah, Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and now Joseph — has died by this final verse, and the family that began as one man called out of Ur has become, by the book's own count in Exodus 1:5, seventy persons settled in a foreign land under a Pharaoh who still remembers Joseph's name. The book closes on that unresolved tension deliberately: a people in possession of a promise they do not yet possess, and a set of bones in Egypt waiting, on Joseph's own recorded expectation, to be carried home.
