Genesis

Chapter Five

King James Version
This is the book of the generations of Adam. In the day that God created man, in the likeness of God made he him; 2Male and female created he them; and blessed them, and called their name Adam, in the day when they were created.

3And Adam lived an hundred and thirty years, and begat a son in his own likeness, after his image; and called his name Seth: 4And the days of Adam after he had begotten Seth were eight hundred years: and he begat sons and daughters: 5And all the days that Adam lived were nine hundred and thirty years: and he died.

6And Seth lived an hundred and five years, and begat Enos: 7And Seth lived after he begat Enos eight hundred and seven years, and begat sons and daughters: 8And all the days of Seth were nine hundred and twelve years: and he died.

9And Enos lived ninety years, and begat Cainan: 10And Enos lived after he begat Cainan eight hundred and fifteen years, and begat sons and daughters: 11And all the days of Enos were nine hundred and five years: and he died.

12And Cainan lived seventy years, and begat Mahalaleel: 13And Cainan lived after he begat Mahalaleel eight hundred and forty years, and begat sons and daughters: 14And all the days of Cainan were nine hundred and ten years: and he died.

15And Mahalaleel lived sixty and five years, and begat Jared: 16And Mahalaleel lived after he begat Jared eight hundred and thirty years, and begat sons and daughters: 17And all the days of Mahalaleel were eight hundred ninety and five years: and he died.

18And Jared lived an hundred sixty and two years, and he begat Enoch: 19And Jared lived after he begat Enoch eight hundred years, and begat sons and daughters: 20And all the days of Jared were nine hundred sixty and two years: and he died.

21And Enoch lived sixty and five years, and begat Methuselah: 22And Enoch walked with God after he begat Methuselah three hundred years, and begat sons and daughters: 23And all the days of Enoch were three hundred sixty and five years: 24And Enoch walked with God: and he was not; for God took him.

25And Methuselah lived an hundred eighty and seven years, and begat Lamech: 26And Methuselah lived after he begat Lamech seven hundred eighty and two years, and begat sons and daughters: 27And all the days of Methuselah were nine hundred sixty and nine years: and he died.

28And Lamech lived an hundred eighty and two years, and begat a son: 29And he called his name Noah, saying, This same shall comfort us concerning our work and toil of our hands, because of the ground which the LORD hath cursed. 30And Lamech lived after he begat Noah five hundred ninety and five years, and begat sons and daughters: 31And all the days of Lamech were seven hundred seventy and seven years: and he died.

32And Noah was five hundred years old: and Noah begat Shem, Ham, and Japheth.

Study: The Weight of “And He Died”

Genesis 5 is the first true genealogy in the Bible, and it reads like one: a name, a lifespan measured in centuries, sons and daughters, a final total, and then the same four words closing it out — "and he died." Adam, Seth, Enos, Cainan, Mahalaleel, Jared: six men, six enormous lifespans, six identical endings. The refrain is almost hypnotic by design. It is what makes the seventh name land the way it does.
Enoch is the seventh generation counted from Adam — and that count matters, because Cain is not in this line at all. Genesis 4 already made the case, from the same source we've been drawing on, that Cain was not Adam's son by nature; this genealogy quietly confirms it in its own way, by never mentioning him.
“
“Enoch was the seventh from Noah [i.e., from Adam]... which was a type of the church ages. Now, all of the rest of the six men, before him, died, but Enoch was translated... showing that it's the seventh church age that takes the Rapture.”
— William Branham, “The Rapture” (65-1204)
Verses 22 and 24 both use the same unusual phrase for Enoch that no other name in the chapter receives: he did not merely live, he "walked with God." The distinction is doing real work. Everyone else in this chapter lived and died on the same terms as the rest of fallen humanity. Enoch's whole life, in the space of two verses, is defined by a relationship rather than a lifespan.
“
“Enoch walked with God five hundred years, and had a testimony that he pleased God. And he was a type of the raptured Church.”
— William Branham, “Hear Ye Him” (60-0313)
Everything else in the chapter simply resumes after him. Methuselah lives longer than any man in Scripture and still dies. Lamech names his own son Noah — "rest," or "comfort" — out loud hoping for relief from a curse that has now weighed on the ground for five generations. It is a small, human moment of hope tucked into a wall of numbers, and it points forward directly into the chapter that follows.

Enoch, and the Riddle in Stone

There is a second thing Brother Branham taught about this same man, entirely apart from his translation, and it deserves its own space here rather than a passing mention: that Enoch, before the flood, built the Great Pyramid of Giza — not as a tomb, but as a memorial and a prophecy carved into stone, meant to survive the very judgment that was coming on the rest of the world he lived in.
“
“Enoch built it before the flood. It was a memorial, that's the reason it never destroyed during the time of the flood... Now if you notice, way down at the bottom it's wide, and then it shapes up a little more in the minority, and then a little more... That's the Lutheran age, justification in the Church; sanctification; then the baptism of the Holy Ghost. But as it shapes towards the top, it continues from Lutheran age, to Wesley age, into Pentecostal age.”
— William Branham, “The True Easter Seal” (61-0402)
The detail Brother Branham kept returning to is the pyramid's missing capstone — still visible today, he'd point out, in the unfinished pyramid on the reverse of the U.S. one-dollar bill's Great Seal. In this reading, the structure itself is a kind of architecture of church history: wide at the base, narrowing upward through age after age, every stone honed and fitted with a precision that still puzzles engineers, waiting on a single stone that was never set.
“
“And notice in the pyramid when he built it, in time of Enoch, every stone was in there. They could measure them things just exactly and tell the wars and things. Everything is complete but the Headstone... It never was capped. Christ is the Headstone that was rejected, He was the rejected Headstone. He's coming back pretty soon.”
— William Branham, “Revelation, Chapter Four, Part III” (61-0108)
It's worth noting that even Brother Branham didn't always present this with the same certainty he gave to the plain text of Scripture. In at least one sermon he set it apart explicitly as something offered for understanding rather than settled doctrine — a distinction worth keeping in mind here, the same way we would for any teaching built more on typology than on a direct verse.
“
“Like Enoch, who built the pyramids, that we believe. And the capstone never was put on top of them, because the headstone was rejected. And we taken now, not as Doctrine, but as to understand, just for the church here.”
— William Branham, “Blasphemous Names” (62-1104M)
Taken together, the two things this chapter's seventh name is remembered for aren't as separate as they first appear. A man who walked with God closely enough to be taken without dying is also, in this telling, a man entrusted with a warning in stone massive and precise enough to outlast a global flood — both pointing at the same event from two different directions: a Church being shaped, generation by generation, toward a Headstone still to come.
Nine men in this chapter live, father children, and die, in that order, without exception. One man walks with God, leaves a monument that has outlasted every empire since, and is simply no longer found. Whatever else Genesis 5 is doing as a record, it is also making a quiet argument in its structure alone: death is the rule everywhere in this fallen line, except wherever a man actually walks with God.
